1. Introduction

    Aviation provides a range of economic and social beneﬁts, ranging from jobs and employment at the local and national level, to
the individual leisure beneﬁts of foreign holidays (Airports Commission, 2013; Caves, 2003; Reynolds et al., 2007). However,
aviation noise is also a leading environmental complaint in the European Union (European Parliament, 2002), and an issue of intense
public debate for residents in aﬀected areas around airports (Howarth and Griggs, 2013). Aviation noise and pollution are associated
with negative health and wellbeing eﬀects (Black et al., 2007; Clark and Stansfeld, 2011; Franssen et al., 2004), and environmental
damages (Wolfe et al., 2014), all of which are associated with lower levels of individual welfare (Frey and Stutzer, 2010; MacKerron
and Mourato, 2009; Welsch, 2006).
    In recent years there have been calls for alternative metrics to the standard monetary national GDP and cost-beneﬁt analysis
(CBA) to be used for estimating the impacts of policies and interventions (Stiglitz et al., 2009). The trend to date in academic and
policy discourse on aviation impacts has focused on objective list measures and preferences, for example noise, health and GDP

(Dolan et al., 2011; Navrud, 2002). However, increasing attention is being placed on subjective wellbeing (SWB) data in policy
decisions and academic research, evidenced by the growing number of academic publications and recent government-level guidance
on using wellbeing metrics in policy evaluation (Fujiwara and Campbell, 2011; OECD, 2013). Traditional preference-based accounts
of welfare are based on the premise that we can infer welfare from looking at people's choices and market behaviour (Parﬁt, 1984).
However, this approach relies on perfect functioning markets to signal the eﬀects that non-market externalities have on welfare. In
contrast, SWB accounts refer to people’s subjective experiences of their own wellbeing. They look at how the individual feels and
thinks about their life, measuring what conditions make people feel their lives are going better or worse. This provides a more direct
account of the eﬀects that a non-market good, like environmental noise, has on peoples’ welfare. In addition, the SWB approach is
particularly attractive because it excludes the possibility of framing eﬀects and response bias that arise when asking people directly
about their experience living with noise (Kahneman and Tversky, 1984).
    To date, few studies have addressed the impacts of airport proximity or noise on SWB, or have been limited to surveying the
impact of aviation at the global or household level on those that live around airports or within aircraft noise contours (Lawton and
Fujiwara, 2016; Navrud, 2002; Van Praag and Baarsma, 2005). This is the ﬁrst study to explore the relationships between airports and
SWB using a large Experience Sampling Method (ESM) dataset for the United Kingdom (UK). The Mappiness survey provides over a
million observations taken in real-time during diﬀerent moments of the day. ESM surveys allow us to explore issues of causality in a
more robust way than standard regression analysis (which is the method typically used to assess the drivers of wellbeing in the
academic literature). This is because our ESM survey collects data from the same individuals over short periods of time (usually
around a few months). The structure of the ESM surveys means that most other drivers of an individual’s wellbeing are controlled for
as individual ﬁxed eﬀects, meaning that we can better single out the impact of an activity or event on someone’s wellbeing. ESM
methods provide the closest estimate to causal eﬀects that can be achieved with wellbeing data, excluding the use of randomised
control trials (Csikszentmihalyi and Hunter, 2003; Stone and Shiﬀman, 1994). This means that the level of causal inference are
stronger than in existing studies of aviation and SWB which use cross-sectional data (Lawton and Fujiwara, 2016; Navrud, 2002; Van
Praag and Baarsma, 2005), providing greater certainty for policy-makers. Furthermore, the Mappiness application provides a more
ﬁne-grained alternative to analyzing SWB than national household datasets as peoples’ responses are more closely aligned with
speciﬁc locations and activities undertaken at the time of the survey.
    Our analysis using the Mappiness data considers three potential impacts: (1) the association between being near an airport and
aﬀective wellbeing (at the time of the survey); (2) the association between being in an airport noise contour and aﬀective wellbeing
(at the time of the survey); and (3) the association between working in an airport and non-work activities (leaving/departing/picking
up or dropping oﬀ people for non-work reasons) and aﬀective wellbeing.
    Mappiness provides a number of insights of relevance for practitioner and policy purposes. Since SWB responses in Mappiness are
more closely aligned with the activity of interest and provide longitudinal data for each individual over the course of a day, we are
able to make more robust inferences about the direction of cause and eﬀect between aviation and SWB (Kahneman et al., 2004). This
gives us a broader insight into the range of impacts, both positive and negative, that airports have on the wellbeing of those in and
around them. It allows us to divide activities within airports between those who work there and those who are passing through for
what we assume are travel purposes, as well as capture the eﬀects of aircraft noise beyond airports, in ways which have not been
explored in previous cross-sectional analysis. This provides important insights on the impacts of aviation noise on wellbeing, and the
aviation features that have most aﬀect, positive and negative, on individual wellbeing. In the ﬁnal analysis, we outline how these
analytical insights may be used to quantify potential mitigation and compensation measures required to oﬀset the impacts of aviation
activities on those whose wellbeing is negatively aﬀected.

2. Literature review

    The use of SWB data for academic and policy purposes has tended to focus on two broad categories of wellbeing (Di Tella and
MacCulloch, 2006; Diener et al., 1999; Dolan et al., 2008; Frey and Stutzer, 2002; Kahneman et al., 2003). Evaluative SWB data, such
as life satisfaction, are usually measured in annual national surveys, such as the Annual Population Survey (APS) in the UK. The most
commonly used measure of evaluative SWB taps into a cognitive assessment of one’s life, which incorporate an assessment of how
well one’s life measures up to aspirations, goals, and peers, as well as a reﬂection on how one feels now (Diener, 1984; Kahneman and
Krueger, 2006).
    The majority of studies on aviation and SWB to date have explored evaluative measures of SWB. Lawton and Fujiwara (2016)
merge English population statistics on 189,162 individuals at the postcode level, provided by the APS, with noise measurement maps
for seventeen English airports to test for associations between aviation noise and airport proximity within 5 km of an airport. They
ﬁnd a consistent negative association between the presence of daytime aviation noise at and above 55 dB and ﬁve SWB measures,
including evaluative measures of life satisfaction, eudemonic sense of worthwhile, and aﬀective measures of happiness (yesterday)
and anxiety yesterday), as well as positive aﬀect balance (PAB). The magnitude of the impact is equivalent to around half the eﬀect of
being a smoker for some wellbeing measures, with a marginal negative association detected with every additional decibel of aircraft
noise. Notably, the study ﬁnds no signiﬁcant association between wellbeing and living in close airport proximity. The study also ﬁnds
no signiﬁcant association between SWB and living within night-time noise contours.
    The Lawton and Fujiwara (2016) study is limited in the geographical level at which it captures the eﬀects of aircraft noise or
airport proximity. Household surveys record SWB at the residential postcode level. Furthermore, data in the APS is cross-sectional,
meaning that it captures a one-oﬀ measure of an individual’s SWB, and is therefore unable to capture factors operating on the
individual at the time the survey is conducted. For this reason, the results of analysis on large-scale national cross-sectional datasets

are expected to be better suited to evaluative measures of wellbeing life satisfaction, than aﬀective measures of wellbeing (happiness
and anxiety/relaxation), which are more variable to momentary environmental conditions (Kahneman et al., 2003; Pavot and Diener,
1993).
    Van Praag and Baarsma (2005) analyze the association between evaluative ‘quality of life’ on a Cantril scale (Cantril, 1966) and
subjective noise perception measured by subjective evaluations on a discrete scale, with objective noise linked at the postcode level
and measured in Kosten units.1 The study surveyed 1400 individuals living within 50 km of the Amsterdam Schiphol Airport. The
authors ﬁnd no signiﬁcant association between wellbeing and objective noise. Subjective noise produced a signiﬁcant and negative
association with wellbeing. They also ﬁnd evidence that non-acoustic factors, like family size and presence of a garden, increased
subjective noise nuisance, and of heterogeneous eﬀects between perceived noise and the presence of noise insulation, which sig-
niﬁcantly reduced impact on life satisfaction.
    Kroesen et al. (2010) used household surveys to assess the association between objective noise variables, noise annoyance and
residential satisfaction in a 25 km radius around Amsterdam Schiphol. In comparison to other environment eﬀects (road traﬃc noise
annoyance, age and neighbor noise annoyance), aircraft noise was a signiﬁcant but weak predictor of residential satisfaction, but
ranked higher than the eﬀect of neighbour noise annoyance. In common with Van Praag and Baarsma (2005), the Kroesen et al. study
was limited to surveys at the household level.
    Aﬀective SWB, is concerned with a person’s feelings ‘in the moment’ and can encompass both positive and negative feelings
(Kahneman et al., 2003; Pavot and Diener, 1993; Schwarz and Clore, 1983). Positive feelings are often measured in terms of hap-
piness and negative feelings about stress and anxiety. Aﬀective wellbeing is typically measured on a more frequent basis than
evaluative measures. The Experience Sampling Method (ESM) (Csikszentmihalyi and Hunter, 2003; Hektner et al., 2007) and Day
Reconstruction Method (DRM) (Kahneman et al., 2004) collect information on people‘s reported feelings in diary or electronic real-
time during selected moments of the day. The ESM approach provides the closest estimate to causal eﬀects that can be achieved with
wellbeing data. This means that the level of causal inference that can be made of the results in this paper are stronger than in existing
studies using cross-sectional data (Lawton and Fujiwara, 2016; Navrud, 2002; Van Praag and Baarsma, 2005) To date no research has
been undertaken on the association between aviation and aﬀective SWB using ESM methods.

3. Methods

3.1. Data

    Mappiness is an iPhone application that permits individuals to record their wellbeing scores on a scale of 0–100 via their phone.
The data contain more than one million observations from tens of thousands of individuals in the UK, collected since August 2010.2
Individuals who have downloaded the application receive randomly timed signals on their phone to request that they complete a very
short survey. The survey asks individuals to rate themselves on three dimensions of momentary wellbeing, stating how happy,
relaxed, and awake they feel (full survey instrument in Appendix A1). We focus on happiness and relaxation in this paper. Each score
is elicited by means of a continuous slider (a form of visual analogue scale — see Couper et al., 2006). The ends of each scale are
labelled “Not at all” and “Extremely”, and an individual positions themselves on the scale by drawing a ﬁngertip across the screen.3
    Individuals are asked whom they are with and can choose either ‘alone’ or as many as apply out of seven other categories: partner;
child(ren); relative(s); peer(s); work client(s); friend(s); other. They are then asked whether they are indoors, outdoors, or in a
vehicle, and whether they are at home, at work, or elsewhere. Finally, they are asked what they were doing “just now”. The
respondent chooses all that apply out of 40 response options, which include a range of cultural activities.4
    Together with the responses to the survey, the application transmits the satellite positioning (GPS) location of the individual and
the precise time at which the survey is completed. It also records the time elapsed between the random signal and response, allowing
analysts to distinguish between immediate responses and delayed responses. We exclude responses made more than an hour after the
signal was sent, on the basis that these no longer represent a suﬃciently random sample of experiences. Individuals complete a short
survey about their personal circumstances, work status and household characteristics when registering for Mappiness.

We can therefore determine whether people are near airports or noise contours when responding to the SWB questions and for those

3.2. Analysis

    We run the following regression models using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS):

         Wit = α it + β1Ait + β2 OAit + β3 Pit + β4 Ait Pit + β5 Xit + εit                                                                                     (1)

where W is wellbeing (happiness or relaxation) measured on a scale of 0–100; the subscripts i and t respectively denote the individual
and time period; A is a vector of airport-related variables; OA is a vector of other activities; and X is a vector of control variables that
is made up of location dummies, time indicators (month, day of week, time of day), weather conditions at time of survey and the
number of responses an individual has given previously.
    Eq. (1) is estimated using individual ﬁxed eﬀects (hence the time subscript t is dropped from α i ). Given the short time periods in
the data, socio-demographic variables that are collected from respondents on registration are in eﬀect time-invariant and hence
controlled for in the individual ﬁxed eﬀects. Standard errors are clustered at the person level to account for non-independent repeat
observations and a robust standard error estimator is deployed to account for heteroscedasticity.
    Due to the observational nature of the data, causation cannot be directly inferred and future research should consider this further.
Impact estimates may be biased to some degree if there are confounding factors that have not been controlled for in the analysis. This
is a risk with any wellbeing analysis using non-experimental data. We provide controls for land cover (using the Generalised Land Use
Database 2005), distance from the coast, region, and day of the week (mid-week, weekend, and Bank Holiday) (MacKerron and
Mourato, 2013).
    Fixed eﬀects estimation allows us to control for all time invariant factors speciﬁc to the individual. ESM methods do not require
the inclusion of sociodemographic controls for time invariant factors like education, health, income. The modelling design focuses the
analysis on time variant factors like respondent activity and location in the moment. There is a growing literature which demon-
strates the explanatory power of ESM methods in the evaluation of momentary wellbeing, showing that wellbeing in the moment is
associated with theoretically consistent drivers (Csikszentmihalyi and Hunter, 2003; Hektner et al., 2007; Kahneman et al., 2004).
The focus of the present study is to analyze the associations between aircraft noise and presence within airports, with momentary
wellbeing.
    It should be acknowledged that the population of Mappiness respondents diﬀers in a number of ways from the population at large;
wealthier people, young people and employed people are over-represented relative to the UK adult population. These issues regarding
sample composition in Mappiness should be taken into account when interpreting and extrapolating the results from this study.
However, Mappiness measures align more closely with the activity of interest and provide longitudinal data for each individual,
allowing us to make more robust inferences about the direction of cause and eﬀect between aviation and SWB (Kahneman et al.,
2004).


3.2.1. Stage 1 - Proximity to airports
    We produced two OLS regression models for happy and relaxed scores where Ait = distance to the 17 English airports (banded
as < 3 km, 3–4 km, 5–5 km, 5–7 km, 7–11 km, and 11–15 km). Responses in the coordinates of the airport polygons were excluded
from all analyses to exclude those who were in an airport, since people inside airports are likely to have diﬀerent experiences – they
may be leaving for or returning from holiday (or picking someone up) and have happiness levels associated primarily with this
(otherwise unobserved) activity.


3.2.2. Stage 2 - Aircraft noise contours
    We produced two OLS regression models for happy and relaxed scores where Ait = a binary variable that equals 1 if the GPS
location of the response falls within an aircraft noise contour (≥57 dB) for LHR, LGW, and STN airports. Again we exclude people
within the airport perimeter.

4. Results

    The population of Mappiness respondents diﬀers in a number of ways from the population at large. As one might expect from a
survey conducted with smartphones, respondents are wealthier than the population at large: the median household income category
is £40,000–£56,000, and the midpoint of this range is approximately double the ﬁgure for the UK as a whole. They are also relatively
young: 66% are aged 35 or under, and 95% are aged 50 or under, compared to 29% and 56% respectively in the UK adult population.
Seventy-seven per cent of participants are in employment and 14% are in full-time education. These groups are over-represented
relative to the UK adult population, in which the proportions are respectively 57% and 4%, primarily at the expense of retired people,
who constitute 1% of Mappiness participants but 22% of the population. Participants’ sex ratio is nearly balanced, at 53% male,
compared to 49% in the UK adult population (MacKerron, 2012) (see Appendix A2 for full description of all variables used in
analysis).

4.1. Stage 1 - Proximity to airports

    We found a signiﬁcant negative association between airports proximity and relaxation (at distance bands of less than 3 km and
between 4 and 5 km) (Table 1). Compared with other activities in the Mappiness dataset the association between airport proximity
and relaxation was slightly less than the negative eﬀect of doing shopping errands (β = −1.294). Being near an airport was not
signiﬁcantly associated with happiness at any distance. We note that in all results reported in this paper, r2 model ﬁt for SWB is within
the range of 10–20% found as standard for SWB regression analysis, as genetic factors may account for as much as up to 80% of the
variance in SWB (Rietveld et al., 2013).

4.2. Stage 2 – Aircraft noise contours

    We found a signiﬁcant negative association between aircraft noise at the 66 dB level and both happiness and relaxation (Table 2).
The negative association between aircraft noise at the 66 dB level and happiness was high compared to the negative eﬀects of an
activity like queuing (β = −3.802) or caring for adults (β = −4.527). The negative association between aircraft noise at the 66 dB
level and relaxation was greater than the negative eﬀects of travelling or commuting (β = −4.087). There was also a statistically
signiﬁcant negative association between happiness and noise at the highest (72 dB) level. However, we disregard this result due to the
small sample of respondents within this noise contour (n = 12). These ﬁndings suggest that there was some association with being in
aircraft noise contours at the time of responding to the Mappiness application, but only at high dB levels.

4.3. Stage 3 - Airport activities

    We did not ﬁnd a statistically signiﬁcant diﬀerence between workers inside and outside of airports for happiness or relaxation.
    As well as assessing the associations between airport proximity and SWB, we are able to explore Mappiness to understand how
people respond to the ESM when located within airports, and how this is associated with the activities undertaken there. We found a
signiﬁcant positive association between being at an airport and happiness (Table 3). The eﬀects are similar to the happiness increases
associated with drinking a tea or coﬀee (β = 1.353) and the happiness associated with watching TV. We found a signiﬁcant negative
association between being at an airport for non-work purposes and relaxation with an eﬀect comparable to more than twice the
negative coeﬃcient for doing housework and chores (β = −3.454). These results suggest that being in an airport is associated with a
sense of happiness and excitement, but that it is also stressful.

5. Discussion

    This study analyzes the relationship between airports and wellbeing using data from the Mappiness iPhone application, a large
experience sampling method dataset for the UK. Analyzing how being within airports or aviation noise contours explains variation in
people’s responses to aﬀective (momentary) SWB is an innovative way of appraising impact at a detailed level of time and location
using a large-scale national data set. We match peoples’ reported SWB to their geolocated positions in airport locations and aviation
noise contours using noise measurement maps from airport operators in England. This is the ﬁrst time that momentary ESM data has
been applied to the aviation sector. The ESM method provides stronger levels of causal inference by controlling for time invariant
ﬁxed eﬀects like health, personality, and sociodemographic characteristics. It also allows the analyst to make stronger links between
the location a person is in and their self-reported wellbeing (Csikszentmihalyi and Hunter, 2003; Stone and Shiﬀman, 1994).

In addition, aircraft noise analysis was restricted to noise contours around three London airports, due to limitations in data available

the Mappiness dataset.
    These ﬁndings have important implications for policy, providing the strongest evidence to date of the negative association be-
tween aircraft noise, airport location, and subjective wellbeing. Where previous cross-sectional studies have identiﬁed a negative
association with life satisfaction at the household level (Lawton and Fujiwara, 2016; Van Praag and Baarsma, 2005), ESM provides
greater robustness and higher levels of causal inference by controlling for a wider range of factors (Csikszentmihalyi and Hunter,
2003; Stone and Shiﬀman, 1994). This provides important evidence of the negative externalities produced by the aviation industry,
not only on those living in the local area, but also those within proximity to airports or aircraft noise.
    Within the data we are also able to explore the reported wellbeing of people within airports within the moment. The relationship
between airport travel, happiness and relaxation is a particularly interesting one, because it is associated positively with happiness
but negatively with relaxation. This is almost a unique ﬁnding in the momentary SWB literature to date (MacKerron and Mourato,
2013). Typically the relationship between perceived stress and happiness has been found to be inverse (i.e., individuals with per-
ceived higher levels of stress report being less happy than those with lower levels of stress, see Schiﬀrin and Nelson, 2008). Yet this
ﬁnding is intuitive, suggesting that airports are a place of pleasure at travelling or holidaying accompanied by the stress of organising
and tight schedules. There was no diﬀerence in happiness and relaxation when comparing people who work in airports against similar
people that work outside of airports. This suggests that the positive association between employment and wellbeing is not weakened
by the job being in an airport.
    These ﬁndings demonstrate a wider perspective that shows the positive impacts that airports have in terms of air travel, leisure,
and employment opportunities, providing a more balanced view for policy-makers. The ability to identify the range of work and non-
work activities associated with wellbeing within airports gives us broader insight into the range of impacts, both positive and
negative, that aviation has on people’s momentary wellbeing.
    A policy-relevant interpretation stemming from the present study is that the negative eﬀects of aviation noise may be oﬀset by
positive aspects of air travel on the economy and leisure. It does not, however, imply that some people will not lose out. Those present
in noise contours and not beneﬁting from the economic or leisure beneﬁts airports provide are likely to suﬀer negative eﬀects on their
SWB due to noise. Standard policy solutions for mitigating the negative eﬀects of aircraft noise have focused on noise insulation
measures to reduce the negative eﬀect of high levels of aircraft noise. An alternative approach to mitigate the negative eﬀects of
aviation noise on wellbeing may involve improving the provision of services that have a known positive eﬀect on SWB. These may
include measures to improve health conditions in the local area, through improved health provisioning services, communication
drives for health education and discouraging smoking, and measures to reduce unemployment in the local area. Improvement to the
local economy is one of the economic factors commonly presented in airport expansion options (Airports Commission, 2014). It is
important to ensure that these opportunities are made available to those in the local community who are most likely to be aﬀected by
increases in air traﬃc and aviation noise.

and some policy-makers have argued that alternative metrics to the standard monetary GDP and CBA measures may be required for
estimating the impacts (positive and negative) of policies and interventions on citizens (Stiglitz et al., 2009). The analysis outlined in
this paper represents an attempt to estimate the impacts of government decisions in one sector – that of aviation policy – using SWB
measures. Going forward, governments may increasingly aim to apply measures such as these to better quantify the level of com-
pensation required to oﬀset the negative eﬀects of aviation activity. This compensation may be calculated in terms of activities that
produce an equivalent uplift in SWB, in non-monetary terms. It could alternatively be calculated in monetary terms by estimating the
compensating level of income required to give individuals the same amount of wellbeing as they would have had without the
experience of aircraft noise/airport location (Dolan and Fujiwara, 2016; Fujiwara, 2013). The advantage of this approach is that it
provides a more direct measure of welfare changes than other forms of monetary valuation, such as revealed preference hedonic
models, which are dependent on proxy markets, such as housing which may be subject to distortions.
    We conclude with some ﬁnal methodological considerations and recommendations for future research. In contrast to existing
studies on aviation noise which have focused on evaluative measures of satisfaction at the household level in the UK, we found that
proximity to airports at less than 5 km had a signiﬁcant negative association with feeling relaxed. This ﬁnding is in contrast to Lawton
and Fujiwara (2016), who found no association between airport proximity and SWB using household surveys. Household surveys are
attached to residential postcodes meaning that respondents with higher tolerance of aircraft noise may have self-selected into the area
(Frederick and Loewenstein, 1999). In contrast, Mappiness respondents are by deﬁnition present within the 5 km airport perimeter at
the time of the survey, but may be non-residents or commuters passing through or using some other facilities around the airport. This
suggests that the reason that an individual is within an airport (the activity they are performing, whether travelling, working, or some
other leisure purpose) is an important factor in the relationship between airports and SWB. More advanced analysis could test what
types of activities are most commonly performed within 5 km of the airport to the wellbeing of residents within the same survey.
Finally, there is room for innovation in this analysis by inclusion of daily or hourly ﬂight path information that could be temporally
linked to the GPS of the Mappiness response. Improvements in this area would require the release of more detailed noise contour data
from the airport operators.
    In sum, this study presents a highly novel approach through its use of ESM methods to assess the associations between aircraft
noise/location and wellbeing, which has not been analysed in the context infrastructure generally, or airport noise speciﬁcally.
Among the advantages of the ESM method is that it allows for more robust levels of causal inference than that involved in the
standard OLS regression modelling commonly used for SWB analysis and focused on peoples’ actual location rather than household
postcode. Our paper makes stronger inferences around the issue of cause and eﬀect between aircraft noise and wellbeing, and so
constitutes a novel addition to the literature in this area.
