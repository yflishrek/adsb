1. Introduction

    This paper sets three novel research goals. First, our analysis shows the performance of airports on several indicators and relates
that to underlying patterns of economic development, airport size and aircraft used, and shows how this has evolved over time. To
this end, we adopt a longitudinal approach (a 10-year time period) to evaluate changes using data from Jiangsu Province (China).
Second, to the best of our knowledge, we examine and classify the spatial patterns of aircraft CO2 emissions at a regional level for the
ﬁrst time, controlling for spatial distribution and autocorrelation. Finally, considering the spatial diﬀerence in the pollution mea-
sures, we provide policy implications to help authorities to further reduce aircraft CO2 emissions. Our research approach is grounded
in that emission reduction and sustainable economic development are both critical challenges for the future of air transport.
    Carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions are produced every day by the burning of fossil fuels, which result from daily human activities
including production and use of electricity, heating, industry, and transport. Increased CO2 emissions contribute to global warming

and climate change. Many research results concluded that the aviation sector represents a small, but potentially signiﬁcant and
increasing factor having a negative impact on climate change (Dessens et al., 2014; ICAO, 2010; IPCC, 1999; Lee et al., 2009; Zhang
and Witlox, 2020). The IPCC (1999) estimated that the aviation sector contributes about 3% to global anthropogenic CO2 emissions in
1992, which might increase to 5% for a mid-range emissions scenario by 2050. At the same time, the aviation sector is also the fastest-
growing mode of transport with passenger demand projected to double by 2035 (IATA, 2016).
    Emission reduction and sustainable development are critical challenges for the future of air transport. Airports, as key nodes in an
air transport system, play a vital role in ground processing and handling of aircraft. These operations consume fuel and emit CO2.
Additionally, and by way of an example, it has been shown that for a modern twin-engine aircraft operating over an 800 km range
ﬂight, about 25% of the emissions are produced during the landing and take-oﬀ (LTO) cycle (Saravanamuttoo et al., 2001). Therefore,
aircraft CO2 emission at the level of the airport deserves speciﬁc attention.
    In response to the increasing concern for aircraft CO2 emissions at airports, a growing body of literature has examined the
amounts and characteristics of aircraft CO2 emissions. The CO2 emissions of aircraft activities at airports have been investigated at a
speciﬁc airport level (Ashok et al., 2017; Postorino and Mantecchini, 2014; Schäfer et al., 2003; Sherry, 2015; Shirmohammadi et al.,
2017; Winther et al., 2006), at a regional/national multi-airport scale (Johansson and Ek, 2003; Song and Shon, 2014, 2012; Stettler
et al., 2011; Wasiuk et al., 2015), and also globally (Kim et al., 2007; Simone et al., 2013; Wasiuk et al., 2015; Wilkerson et al., 2010)
by using diﬀerent techniques and methodologies (Kurniawan and Khardi, 2011; Masiol and Harrison, 2014).
    Apart from the geographical scale (local, regional, global), other impacts have been analyzed as well: i.c., temporal variation
(Song and Shon, 2012; Winther et al., 2006), spatial dispersion (Shirmohammadi et al., 2017; Winther et al., 2006), development
trends (ICAO, 2016; Schäfer and Waitz, 2014; Vedantham and Oppenheimer, 1998), and diﬀerent sources of uncertainty (Lee et al.,
2007; Simone et al., 2013; Stettler et al., 2011; Underwood et al., 2004). Additionally, the main driving factors and reduction
measures of CO2 emissions from aircraft movements at airports have also been widely discussed (Ashok et al., 2017; Evertse and
Visser, 2017; ICAO, 2016; Koudis et al., 2017; Monsalud et al., 2014; Neuling and Kaltschmitt, 2018; Sherry, 2015; Simaiakis, 2009;
Teoh and Khoo, 2016; Xu et al., 2018).
    The overall ﬁnding is that aircraft CO2 emissions are closely related to the aircraft type, the airport capacity, and the geographical
location of the airport.
    Hence, knowing underlying patterns of economic development, airport size and aircraft used, and the characteristics and cor-
relations of aircraft CO2 emissions at diﬀerent airports will contribute to a better understanding of aviation’s impact and how we can
reduce CO2 emissions at these diﬀerent airports within that region. Previous studies have also pointed out that there exist some
diﬀerences in CO2 emissions in the same regional area (Yim et al., 2013; Zhu et al., 2019). Such spatial diﬀerences and correlations
should therefore also be taking into account apart from the temporal analysis.
    In sum, assessment and reduction of CO2 emissions are among the most important issues of low-carbon airport development, and
understanding CO2 emission disparities is crucial to reducing CO2 emissions at that regional level. However, previous research rarely
investigated the spatial characteristics of aircraft CO2 emissions at the regional level. We aim to ﬁll this research gap.
    The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the research methodology. Section 3 presents the research region and data
sources. Section 4 presents our main ﬁndings (spatial distribution, spatial classiﬁcation, and spatial autocorrelation of aircraft CO2
emissions). Section 5 provides a discussion of the policy implications, and Section 6 draws conclusions, discusses research limitations,
and suggests avenues for future research.


2. Research method

    We focus on how spatial characteristics impact aircraft CO2 emission levels. To do this, we ﬁrst calculate the aircraft CO2 emission
according to IPCC and ICAO recommended approaches. This input is then used to analyze the distribution of emission quantity and
intensity, which will give us a general understanding of the spatial distribution of aircraft CO2 emissions. Next, airports are cate-
gorized using classiﬁcation matrices. And, ﬁnally, we look for spatial patterns (i.e., spatial dispersion or aggregation) by applying a
spatial autocorrelation analysis. Fig. 1 visualizes these diﬀerent steps.

2.1. Carbon dioxide emission estimation model

    Aircraft engines emit CO2, H2O, CO, HC, NOX, SOX, and volatile and nonvolatile Particulate Matter (PM) primarily. Typically,
aviation emission consists of 71% CO2 and 28% H2O (Pham et al., 2010). In this study, only the CO2 emission is investigated as the
exhaust of modern aircraft engines has few other greenhouse gases, such as NO2 and CH4 (IPCC, 2006).
    The level of CO2 emissions depends on the composition of carbon and hydrogen in the aviation fuel and the amount of fuel
consumption only; the emission index of CO2 is generally regarded as constant. We follow the method recommended by the IPCC to
calculate the CO2 emissions (IPCC, 2006). The calculation formula is as follows:
         EQit = FQit × NCV × EF                                                                                                             (1)
where EQit is the annual quantity of CO2 emission in year t at airport i (kg); FQit is the annual quantity of fuel consumption in year t at
airport i (kg); NCV is the net caloriﬁc value of aviation fuel (kJ/g); and EF is the emission factor of aviation fuel (ng/J). NCV and EF
are the default values according to the IPCC, 44.3 kJ/g and 70,000 ng/J, respectively.
    To obtain the fuel consumption of aircraft movements at airports, we apply the landing-and-take-oﬀ (LTO) cycle method put
forward by the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO). A typical LTO cycle contains all aircraft operations at/near an
airport below the altitude of 3000ft above airﬁeld elevation, which consists of four operation modes: approach, taxi, take-oﬀ, and
climb. The ICAO also gives engine thrust settings (expressed as a percentage of maximum rated thrust), standard operating time in
each speciﬁc operating mode (Time-In-Mode, TIM), and fuel ﬂow rate of a particular engine type in each operating mode.
    ICAO also provides three general approaches to quantify aircraft engine fuel consumption and emissions— a simple, advanced,
and sophisticated approach—each with a degree of accuracy and an inverse degree of uncertainty. In this paper, the advanced
approach is adopted, according to the availability of detailed and speciﬁc operating data and information.
    The annual quantity of fuel consumption in year t can be calculated by the following formula:

    Having determined the level of CO2 emission, we now want to link this to the airport type. This is done using a spatial distribution
and classiﬁcation methods.

2.2. Spatial distribution analysis

    Spatial distribution analysis refers to how aircraft CO2 emissions are arranged across airports, which could help us to analyze the
relative position of airports within that region. The simplest way to describe the spatial distribution of CO2 emissions is to refer to the
quantity and intensity distribution of CO2 emissions over the geographical area. By means of map overlay the analysis of spatial
distribution patterns of diﬀerent phenomena can be illustrated. If the gradients of distribution are more similar, then the association
is more close, and vice versa.

2.3. Spatial classiﬁcation analysis

    Spatial classiﬁcation analysis is widely applied in data feature identiﬁcation (Oliker et al., 2016; Passarella et al., 2020;
Stuhlmacher et al., 2019; Xing and Meng, 2020; Zou and Greenberg, 2019). We design three diﬀerent classiﬁcation matrices: (i)
Carbon dioxide Emission Classiﬁcation Matrix (CECM), (ii) Economic Development Classiﬁcation Matrix (EDCM), and (iii) Sus-
tainable Development Classiﬁcation Matrix (SDCM).
    In the CECM (as shown in Fig. 2a), we will use two types of indices that are related to aircraft carbon dioxide emissions, one is
linked to emission quantity: CO2 emission quantity — EQ, and another linked to emission intensity: CO2 emission intensity per
passenger — EI. These two indices will be used to classify.
    In the EDCM (as shown in Fig. 2b), we use two common indices which are related to the airport local economy (ICAO, 2002;
InterVISTAS, 2015), one is linked to economic development quantity: Gross Domestic Product — GDP, and the other is linked to
economic development eﬃciency: GDP per capita — GPC.
    Then, we merge these two classiﬁcation matrices into the SDCM (as shown in Fig. 2c). One index combines GDP and GPC, which is
linked to economic impact, and another index combines EQ and EI, which is linked to environmental impact.

2.4. Spatial autocorrelation analysis method

    To investigate whether aircraft CO2 emissions at airports are spatially correlated, we calculated the spatial autocorrelation using
Moran’s I index analysis as put forward by Anselin (1995), Getis and Ord (1992), and Anselin et al. (2013). Moran’s I index analysis is

areas (Cliﬀ and Ord, 1981; Qin et al., 2019; Stuhlmacher et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2017; Zheng et al., 2018). We focus on the spatial
autocorrelation of CO2 emission intensities, and the characteristics of spatial aggregation measured as the spatial dependence degree

    The Moran’s I index is deﬁned over the interval [−1,+1]. Positive values imply positive spatial autocorrelation; that is, a high

random spatial pattern lacking spatial association.

3. Research region and data sources

3.1. Research region

   Our research area is Jiangsu province, located in eastern China. It is one of the fastest growing, aﬄuent and developing regions in
China, with a GDP exceeding 8.5 trillion RMB yuan, ranking it second (after Guangdong) of all provinces in China. Jiangsu province
has nine civil airports (in South Jiangsu: Nanjing Lukou Airport–NKG, Wuxi Shuofang Airport–WUX, Changzhou Benniu
Airport–CZX; in Central Jiangsu: Nantong Xingdong Airport–NTG, Yangzhou Taizhou Airport–YTY; in North Jiangsu: Huai’an
Lianshui Airport–HIA, Yancheng Nanyang Airport–YNZ, Xuzhou Guanyin Airport–XUZ, and Lianyungang Baitabu Airport-LYG, as
shown in Fig. 3). This makes the region to have one of the highest airport densities in China. There exists high airport competition,
with an overlapping catchment area of 71.3% of all domestic routes and 54.3% of all international routes. Additionally, the rapid

carbon aviation, making Jiangsu province a valid research case.


3.2. Data sources

    Diﬀerent data sources are needed. First, all ﬂight schedules were taken from the airline schedules database (www.OAG.com).
OAG maintains an extensive ﬂight status and day-of-travel database. Each record of passenger and cargo ﬂights has comprehensive
information, including ﬂight number, aircraft type, operating carrier, origin airport, destination airport. For our study, all ﬂights of
passenger and cargo ﬂights operated at nine airports in Jiangsu province were collected from January 1st, 2007 to December 31st,
2016. All ﬂights records had been sorted and checked by time and ﬂight number to avoid miscalculations (ﬂight double counting).
    Second, as diﬀerent types of aircraft (engines) have diﬀerent performance on fuel consumption and emissions, identifying the
aircraft/engine combinations is a key factor in calculating the CO2 emissions. Aircraft/engine combinations were collected from the
oﬃcial websites of aircraft manufacturers, airlines, and academic literature. As the same type of aircraft may be installed with
diﬀerent types of engines, even in the same airline, aircraft/engine combinations were too intricate and diﬃcult to determine; hence,
the most typical aircraft/engine combinations from the aircraft manufacturers and airlines were singled out (see Appendix A).
    Third, taxiing time accounts for the biggest proportion during a standard LTO cycle and it could be signiﬁcantly diﬀerent from
airport to airport. We use the data from an Operation Monitoring Center of the Civil Aviation Administration of China (CAAC) airline
on-time database, which covers the actual pushback time from the gate, the wheel-oﬀ time, the wheel-on time, and block-in time at
the gate, and compute the taxi-out and taxi-in time. Due to the data available, we calculated the average taxiing time during January
1st, 2013 to March 31st, 2013 and May 1st, 2016 to October 31st, 2016 at each airport in Jiangsu, and use it instead of the ICAO
default taxiing time in order to improve the calculation accuracy.
    Fourth, statistics of passenger and cargo throughput were extracted from the Statistics Yearbook of China Civil Aviation which
was published oﬃcially by the CAAC. To analyze and contrast CO2 emission intensity clearly and conveniently, passenger and cargo
throughput were converted to throughput by following equations according to the CAAC regulations.

4. Results and discussions

4.1. Spatial distribution analysis of CO2 emission

    Spatial distribution analysis aims to describe the relative position of the airports in the region. Recall that we use two indicators to
measure the spatial distribution analysis of CO2 emission (EQ and EI).
    From the point of view of EQ (Fig. 4a), distinct diﬀerences were observed in Jiangsu province. On average, the EQ of airports in
South Jiangsu (including Nanjing, Wuxi, and Changzhou highlighted in red) was more than 100,000 tons in 2016, which was more
than twice the provincial average, followed by Central Jiangsu (Yangzhou and Nantong, in orange). North Jiangsu (Xuzhou, Lia-
nyungang, Yancheng, and Huai’an) exhibited the lowest CO2 quantity with about 13,000 tons in 2016. Additionally, the features of
EQ in these three parts of Jiangsu province were similar to the geographical and economic characteristics. In brief, the spatial
distribution of EQ decreased from South Jiangsu to Central Jiangsu, then to North Jiangsu. This trend follows the economic de-
velopment trend in Jiangsu province (Fig. 4b), and conﬁrms that air transport is positively related to regional economic development
(Brueckner, 2003; ICAO, 2002). We did a simple correlation analysis; the result showed that CO2 emission quantity had a strong
positive correlation with economic development in Jiangsu province (R2 = 0.98).
    From the point of view of EI (Fig. 5), there were also distinct diﬀerences among the nine airports. On average, EI in South Jiangsu
was about 9.85 kg/pax in 2016, which was the lowest, followed by Central Jiangsu at 10.50 kg/pax. North Jiangsu exhibited the
highest EI, at 11.69 kg/pax, and displayed the opposite trend of CO2 emission quantity. Economies of scale may be the primary cause
for this phenomenon, as the converted throughput per airport in South Jiangsu was 6.73 and 10.05 times more than in Central and
North Jiangsu, respectively.
    Furthermore, economies of scale in airline operations in South Jiangsu are achieved via the operation of larger aircrafts. Facing
booming air travel demand and intercontinental ﬂights, airlines tend to operate bigger aircraft. Airports in South Jiangsu operated

4.3. Spatial autocorrelation analysis of CO2 emission

    In order to study the overall spatial dispersion or aggregation, a global Moran’s I index of the aircraft CO2 emission intensity EI at
airports in Jiangsu province from 2007 to 2016 was calculated by using the software ArcGIS10.0, and the signiﬁcance level of the
calculation results was also tested (Table 2).
    According to Table 2, in terms of EI, the global Moran’s I index was above 0.3 in most of the years, and the corresponding p-values
were less than 0.05, which indicates that positive spatial autocorrelations existed from the point of EI. Therefore, airports with a high
(or low) intensity value often have nearby airports with high (or low) intensity values. It should be noted that the results of 2010 and
2011 failed the test of signiﬁcance. The reason may be that Huai’an Lianshui Airport was put into operation in 2010, and this changed
the spatial distribution of the regional airport layout and also had important impacts on the spatial weights matrix.
    As the global Moran’s I index cannot identify speciﬁc positions of each airport, scatterplots for EI were further applied to study the
spatial dependence.
    As shown in Fig. 10, the spatial aggregation eﬀect in 2007 and 2016 is displayed from the perspective of EI. The scatterplot has
four quadrants formed by the intersection of the horizontal and vertical axes, which represent EI and the corresponding spatial lag,
respectively. The four quadrants in the scatterplot represent the clustering types and depict the following: (1) HH clustering
(quadrant I) —airports with high EI are associated with adjacent airports with high EI; (2) LH clustering (quadrant II) — airports with
low EI are associated with adjacent airports with high EI; (3) LL clustering (quadrant III) — airports with low EI are associated with
adjacent airports with low EI; and (4) HL clustering (quadrant IV) — airports with high EI are associated with adjacent airports with
low EI.
    Furthermore, the airports (e.g., Huai’an, Yancheng, and Lianyungang) that belonged to the HH clustering group were mainly
located in North Jiangsu. The LL clustering group (e.g., Nanjing and Wuxi) concentrated primarily in South Jiangsu. This spatial
aggregation phenomenon was mainly caused by geographical proximity, connected ground transport (road, rail, HSR (see Appendix
B)), airline network, airport size, and economic pattern.
    For airports in South Jiangsu (mainly belonged to the LL clustering group), Nanjing, Wuxi, and Changzhou are well connected by
ground transports: 3 highways, 2 high-speed rail, and 1 traditional rail. Travelers from South Jiangsu can reach each airport within
1.75 h by driving. For the airline network, Nanjing Lukou Airport connected more destinations (Table 3). Taking international routes
as an example, one can ﬂy to Europe, America, Australia, and Asia directly from Nanjing. In contrast, one can only ﬂy to Asian
countries directly from Wuxi and Changzhou. For economic development, South Jiangsu is mainly export-oriented. Lots of people,

    In contrast, for the airports in North Jiangsu (mainly belonged to the HH clustering group), the homogeneity of airline networks

results in forming overlapping catchment areas, increasing competition, and inﬂuencing the activities and performance at these
airports.
    As analyzed above, a spatial aggregation eﬀect between airports in Jiangsu province is observed. This is commonly referred to as a
Matthew eﬀect (Rigney, 2010), implying, “what is already strong gets stronger, what is weak, weaker”. Therefore, collaborations
among these airports and this spatial spillover eﬀect should be taken into consideration in decision-making for low-carbon aviation
development.


5. Policy implications

    Based on the above analyses, we suggest the following policy implications to help authorities (not only in Jiangsu province, but
elsewhere) to encourage low-carbon and more sustainable airport development.

(1) From the perspective of airport authorities, demand management and airport slot optimization should receive more attention.
    Airport authorities are expected to enhance demand investigation with airlines together to expand air travel needs and to catch
    the characteristics of air travel needs (e.g., distribution of peak time of travel, distribution of destinations, etc.). Measures
    combined with airport slot optimization should be taken to ﬁt the temporal characteristics of air travel demand and to achieve a
    balance between ﬂight schedule and such demand (Barnhart et al., 2012) to increase passenger/cargo throughput and load factor.
    As a consequence, high(er) passenger/cargo throughput and load factor may achieve substantial economies of scale (Gillen et al.,
    2016), and will reduce CO2 emission intensity.

    Moreover, as economies of scale play a positive role in reducing airport environmental impact, expanding air transport demand
will help airports obtain this advantage. Airport authorities are suggested to analyze the local economy features, and discover the
roadmap for the movement of people, materials, goods and their evolution trends. Then, airports open new ﬂight routes or adjust
route network according to the local economy characteristics, in order to match air transport and economic development well, and
boost the airport throughput ﬁnally.
    All the above will help airports in the permissible LH (or unwanted HH) group to move to the desirable LL (or permissible HL)
group in the CECM and those airports in the unwanted HL (or permissible HH) group to move to the permissible LL (or desirable LH)
group in the SDCM because of their decreasing environmental impact.

(2) From the perspective of the provincial government, cooperation and coordination mechanisms should be more diﬀerentiated and
    targeted. The provincial government of Jiangsu established the China East Airport Group (EAG) to manage the airports in the
    region in September 2018. As a spatial aggregation eﬀect of airports is observed in Jiangsu province, the spatial spillover eﬀect
    will play an essential role in cooperation and coordination development between airports in the region (Zhang et al., 2019).

    Furthermore, the clustering types of spatial aggregation had a great impact between airports in South Jiangsu and those in North
Jiangsu. The EAG suggested to implement diﬀerent management strategies in diﬀerent regions in the province, according to the
regional characteristic of spatial spillovers eﬀect; and to strike a good/better balance between uniﬁed management of the EAG and
individual features of each airport. Facing an increasing competition between airports, the EAG needs to consider the balance of
airports competing and complementing with each other, in order to improve airport operation eﬃciency. Meanwhile, the EAG should
take neighboring airports in Shanghai into account in the process of administration policymaking.
    Moreover, the government is expected to make full use of the direct, indirect and induced economic impacts of airports which
follow from the circulation of passengers and goods. In this way, it would promote local economic development and help airports in

the permissible LL (or the unwanted HL) group to move to the desirable LH (or the permissible HH) group in the SDCM, ﬁnally
enhance the sustainable development level of these airports.

(3) From the perspective of the national government, at the airport planning and design stage, local economic development and
    spatial eﬀects of nearby airports should be paid more attention. On the one hand, a strong local economy can provide a large
    number of air transport needs and be helpful to achieve the scale eﬀect in airport operation; and on the other hand, considering
    the spatial spillover eﬀect between airports, the national government should take advantage of this eﬀect to optimize the resource
    distribution (e.g., route network, airport slot, etc.) and prevent excessive market overlap between these airports. For example,
    CAAC who represents the national government, approved the only freight ﬂight to South Korea and Japan in Jiangsu Province to
    Yancheng Nanyang Airport, but not to the biggest airport (Nanjing) or the nearest one (Lianyungang), because of the economic
    cooperation between Yancheng City and these two East-Asia countries.

    In addition, the air transport sector will be integrated into the Chinese national carbon trading scheme which had been im-
plemented from the end of 2017. The emission trading scheme is an eﬀective government, market-based approach to limit emissions,
such as the European Union Emissions Trading System (EU ETS), the U.S. Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative (RGGI), etc. And the air
transport sector is recognized as an easy-to-accomplish choice for the carbon trading entity because of its existing good databases,
relative uniformity, and easy measurement and detection. As an example, the air transport sector has been included in EU ETS from
2012 and has a signiﬁcant result for CO2 emission reduction (Anger, 2010; Bel and Joseph, 2015; Vespermann and Wald, 2011).
Thus, for low-carbon aviation development, a carbon trading scheme should be considered.
    Finally, it should be noted that, Jiangsu is located in the plains area with balanced population distribution. The ground transport
is developed with high-speed rails and highways. Moreover, all airports are state-owned, which makes collaborations between air-
ports easier. Therefore, other regions (e.g., Hong Kong-Guangzhou-Macao region) are suggested to consider the above conditions
before they take action.

6. Conclusions

    In airport carbon emissions research, the time dimension has usually been regarded as a key factor and the spatial dimension has
been somewhat neglected. Nevertheless, almost all data with geographic location attributes show some spatial relationships. This
paper calculated the aircraft CO2 emission during LTO cycles at airports in Jiangsu province (China) from 2007 to 2016 using the data
from the Statistics Yearbook of China Civil Aviation, the OAG database, the airline on-time database from CAAC, and the ICAO
Aircraft Engine Emissions Databank. To grasp the performance patterns of airports and spatial characteristics of aircraft CO2 emis-
sions in a region, the spatial distribution, classiﬁcation, and autocorrelation of CO2 emissions was studied, and policy implications for
private and public authorities were provided. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) The spatial distribution features showed that there exist speciﬁc spatial patterns of aircraft CO2 emissions between airports in
    Jiangsu. The distinct spatial distribution diﬀerences were observed from indices of both emission quantity and emission intensity
    per passenger, which can be summarized as emission quantity was “high in South Jiangsu and low in North Jiangsu” and emission
    intensity was the opposite. These spatial distribution patterns conﬁrm that local economic development has a positive relation to
    airport CO2 emission quantity, and the economic scale eﬀects of airport capacity and aircraft type used beneﬁt airports in
    reducing emission intensity. Also, these spatial patterns suggest that the optimization of resources within a region, including
    adjustment of airport slot, reconﬁguration of route network and redistribution of ﬂights, plays an important role in the pursuit of
    low-carbon airports.
(2) The spatial classiﬁcation analysis gave some other interesting results. From the results of the Sustainable Development
    Classiﬁcation Matrix, airport grouping resulted in obvious spatial diﬀerences. The airports in the desirable group are mainly
    located in South Jiangsu, while airports that need to be improved are located in the north. This further reveals the close in-
    teraction between airport CO2 emission and local economic development. Hence, airport and government authorities are ex-
    pected to optimize the network and redistribute the ﬂights to have a better matching between civil aviation and economic
    development, and to achieve the goal of airport sustainable development. In addition, from the classiﬁcation results of the Carbon
    dioxide Emission Classiﬁcation Matrix, most of the airports were categorized into the “high emission intensity” groups, which
    mean low emission eﬃciency. This indicates that these airports have a high reduction potential of CO2 emissions by increasing
    the passenger/cargo load factor.
(3) The spatial autocorrelation analysis displayed the spatial aggregation eﬀect of airports in Jiangsu province. The results of Moran’s
    I index and scatterplots of CO2 emission intensity per passenger showed that the spatial aggregation was signiﬁcant and stable,
    and that spatial diﬀerences were found in most of the years from 2007 to 2016. Also, a so-called Matthew eﬀect was observed.
    More concretely, to keep and strengthen the current good performance of airports in the LL clustering (whose passenger/cargo
    throughput and movements of trunk route aircrafts are usually high), it is suggested that these airports should improve the
    aircraft ground operations (e.g., gate holding strategy, aircraft taxiing route optimization, etc.) in order to cut aircraft emission on
    the ground, and operate low-carbon aircraft to reduce aircraft CO2 emission intensity; while to reduce emission intensity at
    airports in the HH clustering (whose throughput and movements of trunk route aircrafts are always low), these airports are
    expected to focus on a certain sub-market based on their own conditions and advantages (like the freight market to South Korea

                                                                    12
R. Hu, et al.                                                                                   Transportation Research Part D 85 (2020) 102435


     and Japan at Yancheng Nanyang Airport). This measure could help these airports to occupy a dominant position in the sub-
     market and establish competitive advantages. All of this will result in a higher load factor, higher eﬀect of scale and lower
     emission intensity.

    Although the current research has drawn some interesting observations on the spatial characteristics of aircraft CO2 emissions, it
also has some limitations. Due to data limitations, CO2 from terminals and vehicles operated at airports were not assessed in this
study, and ambient meteorological conditions and performance degradation of engines were not taken into account in CO2 emission
calculation. Moreover, additional spatial features, such as how HSR inﬂuences spatial patterns, were not explored in detail. These
limitations need to be explored in the future.
