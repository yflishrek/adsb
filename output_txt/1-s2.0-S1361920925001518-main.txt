1. Introduction

    As urbanization progresses, environmental noise pollution, particularly from airports, has emerged as a major environmental
challenge, significantly impairing the quality of life for residents near airports. Airport noise provokes not only emotional responses
such as anger, disappointment, and dissatisfaction but may also contribute to long-term psychological issues, including depression and
anxiety (World Health Organization, 2011; Vogiatzis, 2014). Additionally, extended exposure to airport noise is associated with
several health risks, including hearing loss (Chen et al., 1997) cardiovascular diseases (Rosenlund et al., 2001), neurological conditions
(Wright et al., 2018), and abnormal fetal development (Nieuwenhuijsen et al., 2017). Residents living in close proximity to the airport
are disproportionately exposed to higher levels of noise pollution compared to other areas, raising concerns about fairness and equality
in noise distribution. Collins et al. (2020) ’s research indicates that there are social inequalities in noise exposure from road and
aviation transport sources. A study has indicated that the duration of living or working in the airport’s environs significantly con­
tributes to annoyance caused by aircraft noise (Faiyetole and Sivowaku, 2021). Consequently, precise assessment of aircraft noise
impact near airports is essential for formulating effective noise management strategies.
    The assessment of aircraft noise impacts begins with the precise calculation of aircraft noise. Aircraft noise modeling employs
various software packages, ranging from comprehensive models that encompass a wide array of parameters to more streamlined
models that exclude certain non-essential parameters. A notable example is the Aviation Environmental Design Tool (AEDT),

developed by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), which integrates multiple tools, including the Integrated Noise Model (INM).
AEDT stands as a prominent model in the field, offering extensive capabilities for assessing environmental impacts, not just noise. It
employs sophisticated algorithms and detailed data inputs to simulate the environmental effects of specific aviation activities.
Additionally, researchers have utilized commercial software such as CadnaA (Vogiatzis, 2011) and SoundPLAN (Ozkurt,2014; Ozkurt
et al., 2015) to generate noise maps for various airports, illustrating the influence of model selection on assessment outcomes. The
technical report ECAC Doc29 (ECAC, 2016), issued by the European Civil Aviation Conference, provides a standardized method for
creating noise contour maps around civil airports. ECAC Doc 29 (ECAC, 2016) highlights the transparency and repeatability of noise
models, enabling various countries and institutions to evaluate and compare noise impacts using common standards. In contrast,
employing the ECAC Doc 29 method for producing noise maps incurs no purchase costs, unlike the other three tools (AEDT, CadnaA,
SoundPLAN).
    Assessing the impact of aircraft noise is essential, particularly in verifying the accuracy of computational models through precise
field measurements. Various studies have utilized diverse noise measurement methods and tools to ensure the reliability of noise
models at different airports. For example, research at Hong Kong International Airport utilized the H4n Pro professional microphone to
record noise during aircraft takeoffs and landings, confirming that discrepancies between measured and predicted noise were within
acceptable limits (Wu et al., 2024). Similarly, at Singapore Changi Airport, researchers implemented a customized smartphone
application to gather detailed data on aircraft noise, showcasing the effectiveness of portable technology in noise assessment (Lee et al.,
2022). At Heathrow Airport, the study integrated the AEDT noise model with real noise measurements using the Noise Monitoring
System (NOMOS), emphasizing the importance of thorough data collection across various flight phases (Giladi and Menachi, 2024).
Additionally, in Athens, following new Greek legislation and EU directives, strategic noise maps and field measurements were
employed to validate noise models and action plans, using the NOMOS system for real-time noise monitoring (Vogiatzis, 2014).
Pradeep and SM (2024) used mobile monitoring technology to evaluate the variations in environmental noise pollution in Chennai,
including the airport area. At Amsterdam Schiphol Airport, a comparison of long-term field measurements with the Dutch aircraft
noise model and NOMOS highlighted yearly variations and enhancements in noise prediction accuracy (Simons et al., 2022). These
studies highlight the range of sophisticated measurement tools—from high-end microphones to IoT-based applications—and stress the
crucial role of integrating field data with computational models to efficiently manage and mitigate the environmental impact of airport
noise.
    Based on these findings, this study adopts the standardized methodology of ECAC Doc 29 to generate airport noise maps and
employs field measurements to validate the accuracy of the computational methods, ensuring transparency and reproducibility in
noise assessments. This approach not only ensures transparency and reproducibility in noise assessments but also provides a cost-
effective evaluation tool, more accurately reflecting the actual impact of aircraft noise on the surrounding environment.
    After calculating aircraft noise, the subsequent critical step involves assessing its impact on the environment and population.
Scholars typically analyze various noise levels’ impact zones (Ozkurt, 2014), the number of people affected by airport noise (Ozkurt,
2014; Ozkurt et al., 2015; Wolfe et al., 2017; Bravo-Moncayo et al., 2019; Zijadić et al., 2022), the count of individuals experiencing
annoyance (Zijadić et al., 2022; Ho-Huu et al., 2019; Ganić et al., 2023), and also consider the social costs of noise pollution (Andreana
et al., 2024). Traditional noise impact assessment methods often merge annual aircraft noise data with census information to produce
noise contour maps. These assessments presume that residents predominantly remain at home, which means that noise mitigation
strategies based on this approach may not accurately reflect the real-life experiences of residents near airports or those who travel to
and from the area for work and other purposes.
    To capture population movement and activity patterns, Aalmoes et al. (2020) developed a mobile application named AnimApp,
which tracks approximate locations to elucidate clear movement patterns, thereby offering a realistic understanding of daily noise
experiences and presenting a novel field research tool. Marquart et al. (2021) explored cyclists and pedestrians perceived and
measured exposure while on the move through a combination of mobile interviews (Go-/Ride-Alongs) and wearable sensors. Ganić
and Babic (2017) were the pioneers in incorporating the dynamics of population movement into the assessment of aircraft noise
exposure. Ho-Huu et al. (2019) hypothesized daily population movements and introduced a new air traffic allocation model that
considers this activity, specifically designed to minimize noise exposure for individuals or groups commuting to the same location
during the day. A case study at Belgrade Airport in Serbia confirmed the reliability and applicability of this approach. Ganić et al.
(2023a) employed an activity-based model and demonstrated that by generating dynamic noise maps, the estimated total number of
people highly annoyed by aircraft noise increased by 10.9 % compared to static noise maps based solely on census data. Ganić et al.
(2023b) used service-level mobile internet traffic data as an approximation of the actual population of a region over a period of time,
and analyzed the noise impact of five routes on the population. The work of Ganić et al. (2023a) has greatly inspired this study,
particularly through their innovative use of dynamic noise maps based on daily mobility patterns to assess noise exposure at Heathrow
Airport. Their research represents a major advancement in understanding how people’s movements throughout the day can affect
noise exposure. Building on their approach, our study introduces a new layer by employing real-time dynamic data rather than
modeled mobility patterns. Unlike the study by Ganić et al. (2023b), which focused on a subset of flights, our research evaluates the
impact of all flights throughout the day on individuals. By using real-time data, this research aims to enhance the precision of noise
impact assessments, providing deeper insights into population dynamics and exposure.
    This study introduces an approach to aircraft noise assessment research by utilizing dynamic population data. Unlike traditional
methods, dynamic assessment models that leverage actual people’s activity and location data more precisely capture the noise
exposure levels experienced by populations at various times and locations. This method surpasses the limitations associated with
speculative or census-based data, offering a direct and empirical method to assess the impact of noise on residents’ quality of life. By
employing dynamic population data, this study not only enhances the accuracy of noise assessments but also sheds light on the

2. Methodology and materials

2.1. Study area

    This research focuses on Shenzhen Bao’an International Airport, a major aviation hub in southern China located 32 km from
downtown Shenzhen. The airport features advanced facilities and an extensive network of routes, with two terminals and two runways
measuring 3,400 m and 3,800 m, respectively. It accommodates both large passenger and cargo aircraft. Bao’an Airport hosts a range
of domestic and international carriers, including aircraft models such as the Boeing 777, Airbus A330, Boeing 737, and Airbus A320. In
2023, it handled 52.73 million passengers and recorded 393,000 aircraft movements, averaging 1,076 flights per day.


2.2. Data collection

2.2.1. Air traffic data
    To estimate the noise generated by aircraft operations around Shenzhen Bao’an International Airport, we systematically collected
flight information on two specific days: December 19, 2023, and February 23, 2024. On December 19, 2023 (Tuesday), the tem­
perature ranged from 11 ◦ C to 16 ◦ C with clear skies and north winds at speeds of 1.7 to 3.1 m/s. On February 23, 2024 (Friday), the
temperature ranged from 15 ◦ C to 20 ◦ C with overcast skies and north winds at speeds of 3.2 to 5.4 m/s. On the first day, we recorded
data for 1,082 flights over 24 h, and on the second, data for 51 flights were collected. We utilized RTL-SDR hardware (Fig. 1) to capture
ADS-B data from aircraft and RTL1090 software to decode the received signals, thereby obtaining detailed information on aircraft
positions, speeds and models. ADS-B data does not directly provide engine information. We obtained the corresponding engine in­
formation for the aircraft based on the provided ICAO code from the ANP data.
    Additionally, to visualize aircraft positions, we employed the Virtual Radar Server (VRS), an open-source software that displays this
information on a map based on ADS-B data, as depicted in Fig. 2. This approach enhances our data collection capabilities, offering an
effective and economical means to gather the ADS-B messages essential for our noise modeling.

2.3. Calculation of noise maps

    The crucial aspect of calculating noise levels at various locations is selecting an appropriate noise model. The ECAC Doc 29
technical report offers a standardized method tailored for creating noise maps around civil airports. Consequently, this study pre­
dominantly utilizes ECAC Doc 29, incorporating model input data that includes daily air traffic information, such as aircraft type,
location, speed, altitude, and times of departure and arrival. Meteorological data, including wind speed relative to the aircraft, air
pressure, temperature, and humidity, are also essential due to their influence on the noise contour. The meteorological parameters we
referenced are as follows: temperature 15 ◦ C, average wind speed of 2.5 m/s (December 19, 2023) and 4.4 m/s (February 23, 2024),
wind direction 0 degrees (north wind), atmospheric pressure 101.325 kPa, and relative humidity 75 %.
    The process of noise map calculation is illustrated in Fig. 4. It begins with the collection and processing of input data, including data
simplification, operation identification, and Aircraft Noise Performance (ANP) matching. The flight path geometry and thrust profiles
are then defined, which are critical for accurately modeling the noise emissions of each flight. Since thrust data is typically confi­
dential, the method for calculating aircraft thrust can be referenced from Appendix B of ECAC Doc 29 Volume 2 (ECAC, 2016a). Noise
levels for each flight are calculated using the Noise-Power-Distance (NPD) tables, with adjustments for factors such as lateral

3. Results

3.1. Verification of the accuracy of ECAC model

    To validate the accuracy of the aircraft noise model for Shenzhen Bao’an International Airport, this study carried out a noise
monitoring experiment near the airport on February 23, 2024, adhering to the standard “Measurement of aircraft noise around air­
ports” (Ministry of Environmental Protection of the People’s Republic of China , 1988). Given the potential for external disturbances,
four monitoring locations with low road traffic flow were strategically selected to primarily capture noise emanating from aircraft.
Fig. 6 illustrates the distribution of these monitoring points. The monitoring utilized the AWA6228 + sound level meter, which records
noise data every second. This device was positioned in open, flat terrain, elevated 1.2 m above the ground, and situated at least 1 m
away from any potential reflecting surfaces to avoid interference. Monitoring points 1 and 2 are located approximately 1.3 km from the
runway 16/34 (west runway), while points 3 and 4 are roughly 1.5 km from the runway 15/33 (east runway). Measurement points 1
and 2 primarily monitor landing aircraft on the west runway (with landings from south to north), while measurement points 3 and 4
primarily monitor departing aircraft on the east runway (also from south to north). During the monitoring process, the direction of
aircraft takeoffs and landings at the airport did not change. Fig. 7 shows the scenario when aircraft pass over monitoring point 4.
    In this verification experiment, we collected noise data from a total of 51 flights, encompassing both landings and departures.
Specifically, monitoring points 1 and 2 recorded noise data for 5 and 15 landing flights, respectively, while points 3 and 4 captured
data for 19 and 12 departing flights, respectively. Upon comparing the model-calculated values with the actual measurements, the
average absolute errors were found to be 1.75 dB for landing flights, 2.27 dB for departing flights, and 2.07 dB overall, thus meeting the
requirement for an error margin within 3 dB (Ministry of Environmental Protection of the People’s Republic of China, 2023). Detailed
verification results can be found in Appendix A. Appendix B presents the specific monitoring details of 12 flights, with 3 flights
monitored at each measurement point. The aircraft types were primarily A321, 737–800, and A320, which had higher occurrence
proportions on December 19, 2023, accounting for 16.5 %, 35.7 %, and 22.1 %, respectively. These findings validate the accuracy of
our method for creating noise contour maps, providing a dependable foundation for further analysis.
    It should be noted that the monitoring of aircraft noise at the four measurement points was not conducted simultaneously. Even for
the measurement points that are close to each other, such as monitoring point 1 and monitoring point 2 (separated by 165 m), as well
as monitoring point 3 and monitoring point 4 (separated by 137 m), the measurements were not taken at the same time. The

3.2. The overall impact of 24-hour airport noise

    On December 19, 2023, Shenzhen Bao’an International Airport managed 1,082 flights. Throughout the day, aircraft took off and
landed from south to north, as shown in Fig. 8. A total of 83 flights took off and 401 flights landed on the west runway (16/34), while
449 flights took off and 149 flights landed on the east runway (15/33). As shown in Fig. 9, in areas close to the airport (around 10 km),
departing flights exhibit relatively diverse trajectories in the vertical profiles, while arriving aircraft generally follow specific landing
procedures, with more conservative flight paths.
    The noise distribution from these flights is represented on the 24-hour equivalent continuous sound level map in Fig. 10, where
darker colors signify higher noise levels. The area with noise levels exceeding 40 dB spans 290 square kilometers. Notably, in the
Bao’an District, locations such as Fuhai Town and Fuyong Town register noise levels ≥ 45 dB, indicating significant noise pollution in
certain residential areas near the airport. These findings highlight the necessity of optimizing airport operations and implementing
effective noise mitigation strategies to minimize the impact on nearby communities, particularly in densely populated streets and
residential zones.
    Given the airport’s geographical layout, with the western side bordered by water and the eastern side urbanized, the use of the
western runway (16/34) may help mitigate the noise impact on urban areas. However, while utilizing a single runway configuration,
specifically the western runway, could reduce noise exposure in nearby residential zones, this configuration may not sufficiently meet
operational needs related to aircraft scheduling, safety, and efficiency. Aircraft departing or landing on the eastern runway (15/33),
which predominantly affect the urban areas to the east, may lead to higher noise exposure in these zones, particularly during peak
operational hours. Therefore, while the western runway may offer noise reduction benefits, relying solely on it could present oper­
ational constraints, requiring a balanced consideration of noise reduction and operational efficiency.

proportion from Fig. 15, we note that the total number of flights and the ≥ 45 dB impact areas at 3 AM, 4 AM, and 5 AM remain

4. Discussion

4.1. The comparison between dynamic and static methods

    In this study, we developed dynamic noise maps and assessed population exposure using dynamic population data. To compare the
dynamic and static methods, we incorporated static population data for Shenzhen. The data is derived from the 100 m gridded
population dataset based on ensemble learning and geospatial big data, as part of China’s Seventh Population Census, published by
Chen et al. (2024). According to Chen et al. (2024) ’s study, experimental results show that their proposed approach provides superior
performance compared to individual base models. Additionally, the out-of-sample town-level test set reveals that the estimated
gridded population dataset (R2 = 0.8936) is more accurate than the existing WorldPop (R2 = 0.7427) and LandScan (R2 = 0.7165)
datasets for China in 2020.
    We calculated the noise-to-static population exposure ratio for each hour of the day using the hourly noise maps. Fig. 16 displays

explanation for this result is that, as a major transportation hub, airports have high activity levels in their surrounding and internal

explained by the following: At 2 AM, the airport’s noise impact is relatively limited, affecting fewer residential areas, resulting in a
lower static exposure proportion (compared to most other time periods during the day). Meanwhile, the dynamic exposure proportion

4.2. The impact of fleet composition on exposure proportions

    In analyzing exposure proportions, we must consider not only the number of flights in each time period but also the fleet
composition. A fleet composition with more flights that produces less noise may result in a lower exposure proportion compared to a
composition with fewer flights but higher noise emissions. Simons et al. (2022) also used a similar method to explain the noise
reduction at Amsterdam Airport Schiphol. Fig. 17 illustrates the fleet composition at Shenzhen Bao’an Airport on December 19, 2023.
To compare the differences in exposure proportions resulting from different fleet compositions, we selected the data for 12 AM and 8
AM, as both time periods have the same number of flights.

composition of the fleet directly influences the noise levels of the fleet, which in turn affects the number of people exposed to different
noise levels. As fleet composition changes, particularly with the introduction of quieter aircraft types, the noise exposure proportion
and exposure intensity may experience significant changes over a longer time scale. Therefore, fleet composition could be an important
factor in mitigating the noise impact on the population.

noise on surrounding residents and enhance their quality of life.

the implementation of differentiated compensation strategies. For example, individuals who are exposed to noise over the long term
should receive higher compensation than those who are temporarily affected. Such an approach ensures that noise management
policies are both equitable and responsive to the actual conditions experienced by the community.

6. Conclusion

    This study conducted a detailed analysis of noise conditions at Shenzhen Bao’an International Airport on a typical workday. Using a
dynamic assessment method, we calculated noise exposure proportions and provided several recommendations for future airport noise
management.
    The airport’s noise maps were generated using the calculation method from ECAC Doc 29, and the accuracy of this method was
validated with a mean absolute error of 2.07 dB, demonstrating the reliability of the results.
    We employed dynamic population distribution data provided by Baidu Huiyan and performed overlay analysis to calculate the
dynamic noise exposure proportions. For noise levels of ≥ 45 dB, the exposure proportion peaked at 14.2 % at 5 PM and decreased to a
minimum of 2.4 % at 4 AM. Additionally, static exposure proportions were obtained using census data, allowing for a comparison. The
results showed that throughout the day, the dynamic proportion consistently exceeded the static proportion, with the largest difference
occurring at 2 AM, where the dynamic proportion was approximately 30 % higher than the static.
    We also analyzed fleet compositions during two specific periods (12 AM and 8 AM) and evaluated the noise emissions of repre­
sentative aircraft for each period. Our analysis revealed that differences in fleet composition, despite identical flight volumes and
population distributions, were a key factor in the variation in exposure proportions.
    Based on our findings, we propose several recommendations for Shenzhen Bao’an International Airport. Although this study only
analyzed a single day, the assessment method described in this paper can be applied for comprehensive noise exposure analysis and
noise reduction strategies, making it particularly useful for policymakers.
    This work advances the field by introducing a dynamic approach to noise assessment that leverages real-time data, offering a more
accurate reflection of population exposure compared to traditional methods. By focusing on actual population movements rather than
modeled assumptions, this research enhances the understanding of how noise impacts communities throughout the day. Future studies
could utilize more refined data, such as mobile signaling data, to calculate the number of people exposed to airport noise, thereby
