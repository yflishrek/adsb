1. Introduction

   The contribution of aviation to anthropogenic climate change has been estimated to 1.65 % in greenhouse gas (GHG) emission
terms (IPCC, 2022) or 3.5 % in terms of effective radiative forcing (Lee et al., 2021). Despite this, the rate of emissions from air
transport has consistently outpaced the overall global emissions growth, largely due to the strong correlation between air traffic
expansion and the socioeconomic development of nations (IPCC, 2022, Lamb et al., 2021). As a result, over the past decade, air travel
has been increasingly pointed out as an excessively harmful activity regarding climate impact. The climate policy challenge of aviation
can be summed up as follows. Firstly, in technological terms, aviation is the least fuel-efficient transportation mode, and, in contrast to
other modes, non-CO2 effects dominate its climate impact (Lee et al., 2021). Secondly, the industry has no reliable short- or medium-
term solutions to increase fuel efficiency significantly or reduce GHG emissions, at least at a cost that is compatible with airlines’ low-
profit margins (Gössling and Humpe, 2024; Huwe et al., 2024) or with the consumer and societal expectations, as higher costs would be
passed through to the consumer (Fichert et al., 2020b). Also, gains in fuel efficiency are offset by traffic growth (Budd and Suau-
Sanchez, 2016; Peeters et al., 2016; Baer, 2020; Gössling and Lyle, 2021; Dobruszkes and Ibrahim, 2022). Thirdly, from the

increases with income (Gössling and Humpe, 2020; Büchs and Mattioli, 2021; Büchs and Mattioli, 2022; Hopkinson and Cairns, 2021;
Zheng & Rutherford, 2022), being disproportionate at the top-end if we consider celebrities and private jet usage (Gössling, 2019;

    In this context, many experts and some policymakers argue that the only efficient policy available in the short term would be
curbing demand for air travel or, in other words, demand management strategies. However, such an option is often considered taboo
when discussed at all (Gössling and Cohen, 2014; Köves and Bajmócy, 2022). There are also concerns that flight- and carbon-intensive
social and economic practices are becoming locked-in (Mattioli, 2016, 2020). Hence, most existing policies are restricted to funding
research on technological solutions, trust in emission trading schemes, and some limited attempts to discourage air travel.
    An example of the latter is how, in France, following a Citizens’ Assembly petition, a legislative ban on short-haul domestic flights
was implemented on 23 May 2023 (Euronews, 2023). This regulation prohibits flights for which there is a train alternative of less than
two-and-a-half hours and which are not connected to long-haul flights. Consequently, this decree impacts a limited number of routes,
such as those from Paris Orly to Bordeaux, Nantes, or Lyon, resulting in a modest effect on reducing greenhouse gas emissions. Recent
studies have shown that the fuel or GHG savings from short-haul flight bans are limited (Baumeister, 2019; Dalkic et al., 2017;
Robertson, 2013; Wang et al., 2019; Avogadro et al., 2021; Reiter et al., 2022). Indeed, as noted by Dobruszkes et al. (2024), 82 % of
fuel consumption occurs in flight sectors longer than 1,000 km, where high-speed trains cannot easily replace air travel. Therefore,
while substituting short-haul flights with high-speed trains may convey a strong societal message, it may also divert policy efforts from
achieving more significant emission reductions.
    Further airport capacity is added every year. On the one hand, many new airports have been built over the past decades, especially
in Asia (e.g., Beijing Daxing International Airport) and the Middle East (e.g., Istanbul Airport). In the EU-27, twelve new airports for
civil aviation have been opened since 1995 (Rudolph et al., 2023). On the other hand, many existing airports have benefited from on-
site expansion to accommodate more traffic (Rodrigue et al., 2017). In the EU-27, ten new runways have been inaugurated since 1995
(Rudolph et al., 2023). Recent examples include Hong Kong Airport (third runway opened in 2022 and ongoing terminal expansion),
New York JFK Airport (where several terminals are being expanded or rebuilt), Frankfurt (fourth runway in 2011, new terminal being
built) and Dublin (second runway opened in 2022), among many others. Further projects to be confirmed include Vienna (third
runway) and Prague (third runway), among many others.1 In the UK, for example, there is an extensive list of planned expansions,2 but
at the same time, the UK Climate Change Committee (CCC), in their Sixth Carbon Budget, required under the Climate Change Act,
argued there should be no net increase in the UK’s airport capacity. In the 2023 progress report to Parliament, the CCC stated that
“there has been continued airport expansion in recent years, counter to our assessment that there should be no net airport expansion
across the UK” (CCC, 2024).
    Along similar lines, a growing number of scholars and NGOs are calling for a moratorium on airport expansion (Element Energy,
2022; Gössling and Lyle, 2021; Greenpeace, 2023; Kuhnhenn et al., 2023; Stay Stay Grounded, 2019; Transport & Environment, 2023).
Recent research conducted in six European countries (including the Netherlands) finds that the public broadly supports stopping the
expansion of airports but is divided on whether this would effectively reduce aviation GHG emissions (Hodgson, 2024). It has also been
suggested that concerns about the climate are one of the factors behind European policymakers’ reluctance to expand airports since the
COVID-19 pandemic (Cagney, 2024).
    Against this background, the Dutch Government caused a major stir when it announced, on June 24, 2022, a decrease in the
number of available slots at Amsterdam Schiphol Airport from 500,000 in 2022 to 440,000 from November 2023 onwards. Note,
however, that at that moment, the airport was operating under that threshold, but 500,000 slots were the historically agreed oper­
ational limit for the airport. The original main motivation for this policy decision was, in fact, related to noise pollution, not GHG
emissions (Government of the Netherlands, 2022). The Government’s proposal arose from a situation of legal uncertainty. Under the
EU Habitat Directive, Schiphol, like other companies in The Netherlands, requires an ecological permit to safeguard nature from
various externalities, including nitrogen deposition. However, the process of securing this permit has proven to be highly complex.
Without an ecological permit in place, the airport traffic decree, which underpins the enforcement system for regulating maximum
noise nuisance, cannot be established. This lack of a legal foundation left the Government with no choice but to revert to the most
recent noise regulation, the Airport Traffic Decree of 2008, to restore legal certainty. Having said that, the Government also viewed the
slot reduction as an opportunity to balance air transport activity with citizens’ quality of life while reducing GHG emissions—a
symbolic and concrete step towards greener aviation policies. The Governmental Letter announcing the slot decrease (Ministerie van
Infrastructuur en Waterstaat, 2022) emphasised that fewer aircraft movements would result in reduced noise pollution as well as lower
emissions of CO2, nitrogen, (ultra)fine particles, and other harmful substances. Greenpeace echoed this perspective, highlighting the
multidimensional benefits of the measure. Their aviation expert remarked, “Schiphol is finally shrinking; this is a historic break­
through. (…) Fewer flights are desperately needed for our nature, health, and the climate. (…). This is a boost for Schiphol to finally
come up with a plan that takes the Paris Climate Agreement into account” (Karst, 2022).
    Assertions around the impact of a slot limit on emissions are challenging (see Niemeier and Forsyth, 2024), as emissions depend not
only on the volume of flights but mainly on the aircraft type and distance flown (Dobruszkes et al., 2024). Hence, in this paper, we first
contribute by analysing potential trajectories of slot reduction in saving emissions, considering different risk assessment scenarios for

2. Literature review

2.1. The relationship between airport slots, distance and aircraft type

    Airport slots are allocated and managed following the Worldwide Airport Slot Guidelines (WASG), a non-binding global standard.
In Europe, slot allocation is regulated by Regulation 793/2004, based primarily on WASG. The process divides the year into summer
and winter seasons. Airlines declare their slot preferences in advance, and an independent coordinator allocates slots within the
airport’s capacity based on transparent and non-discriminatory principles. Historical rights, commonly known as ‘grandfather rights’
are recognised: if an airline has used a series of slots (≥5) for at least 80 % of the previous equivalent season, it retains them for the next
season. Unused slots revert to the coordinator’s pool for reassignment. Up to 50 % of this pool is reserved for new entrants, with the
remainder for incumbents. The coordinator may use additional criteria, such as Public Service Obligations routes or year-round
services, to allocate slots.
    The slot regulation was designed to manage limited capacity at congested airports, especially Level 3 airports requiring year-round
slot coordination.3 In Europe, 70 % of seat capacity and flights are at Level 3 airports, compared to 40 % globally (van Houten and
Burghouwt, 2022). The congestion at European airports has caused some limitations of the Slot Regulations in the form of inefficient
use of slots or reduced competition. Inefficiencies include slot hoarding —holding slots to prevent competitor entry— and slot ba­
bysitting, where poor slot use aims to transfer slots to another airline (EC, 2011).
    Another common effect of the Slot Regulation in congested airports is the phenomenon known as “up-gauging,” where airlines shift
their operations from smaller to larger aircraft and from short-haul to long-haul services. This shift is driven by the high opportunity
costs associated with operating in capacity-constrained airports as slot rents increase (Forsyth et al., 2023). Since airport slots are
scarce and highly valuable, airlines aim to maximise the revenue generated per slot. Larger aircraft and long-haul routes typically offer
higher revenue potential compared to smaller aircraft or shorter routes, as they can carry more passengers and often yield higher ticket
prices. However, this strategic adjustment by airlines often comes at the expense of the diversity of destinations served. As airlines
prioritise routes that generate the most profit, they may reduce or eliminate short-haul flights in favour of fewer, but longer-haul
services. Consequently, the overall number of destinations accessible from the airport diminishes. Additionally, the shift to longer
routes with larger aircraft increases the average distance flown per flight departing from the airport (Gudmundsson et al., 2014; SEO
and Cranfield University, 2017).
    This dynamic has implications not only for the airport’s connectivity but also for its environmental impact, as these longer-haul
flights tend to generate higher GHG emissions due to greater fuel consumption per flight segment. Several authors emphasise that
distance and aircraft type are crucial factors influencing aviation emissions (e.g., Burzlaff, 2017; Seymour et al., 2020; Dobruszkes
et al., 2022; Dobruszkes et al., 2024). Despite their frequency and low fuel efficiency, short-haul flights contribute less to total
emissions due to lower fuel consumption per flight. In contrast, long-haul flights, although fewer in number, have significantly higher
total emissions because they burn more fuel. Aircraft type also impacts emissions, as different models have varying fuel efficiencies,
depending on weight at take-off and on when the aircraft was designed. The authors reveal a strong correlation between seat-
kilometres and fuel burnt, indicating that longer distances and less efficient aircraft lead to higher emissions.


2.2. The case study of Amsterdam Airport and greenhouse gas emissions

   Amsterdam Schiphol Airport has traditionally engaged in active communication with local stakeholders to keep noise under
control. This has historically been an important success strategy of the airport, as citizens have been particularly vocal about the
importance of quality of life and low stress levels (Suau-Sanchez et al., 2011; Burghouwt and Dobruszkes, 2014). In that regard, since
2008, the airport operation has been limited to 500,000 movements as part of the agreement with the local stakeholders in the Alders
Table and, since 2003, the ‘Schiphol Act’ regulated the airport planning around the airport and the air traffic in the area of safety and
noise contours (de Jong, et al., 2008). The current Vision 2030, developed and published by Schiphol Group4 before the COVID-19
pandemic, constitutes a commitment to the 500,000 movements limit and improving the quality of the living environment locally
and globally. The discussions after the COVID-19 pandemic to further limit the airport slot capacity (see Fig. 1 for the movement

different airline reactions and scenarios, as well as the challenges of implementing flight reductions and complementary strategies.

3. Methodology: data, methods and scenarios

3.1. Aviation data and fuel burn model

    Our method required data on airline schedules, which came from the OAG Schedules Analyser dataset and covered the year 2019,
which was the year of peak airport activity as airport traffic continued recovering after COVID-19. The OAG dataset includes infor­
mation on the airlines’ supply of flights, including variables like aircraft model, aircraft seat capacity, number of movements, and great
circle distance flown between the origin and destination.6
    In this paper, following previous studies (e.g., Dobruszkes et al., 2022; Dobruszkes et al., 2024), fuel burnt is considered as a proxy
for GHG emissions and climate change impact.7 In general, the longer the flight and the heavier the aircraft, the higher the fuel burnt.
The literature agrees that this relationship is roughly linear with some slight variations due e.g., to differences in aircraft weight.
Burzlaff (2017) concluded that some variations are near the aircraft’s range limit. Similarly, Park and O’Kelly (2014) detect range limit
variations for wide-body aircraft. Filippone et al. (2021) and Filippone and Parkes (2021) consider that variations depend on atmo­
spheric conditions, flight altitude, payload and other factors. Finally, Seymour et al. (2020) consider that the relationship follows a
slightly quadratic function, and Turgut et al. (2019) note higher dispersion for longer distances.
    Simplified models of fuel of fuel burnt and/or of GHG emissions are based on aircraft type and distance flown. Dobruszkes et al.
(2022) thoroughly reviewed the available models to calculate fuel burn and compared different distance-fuel burnt curves. These are
the EMEP/EEA Air pollutant emission inventory (Baumeister and Leung, 2021), the Eurocontrol Small Emitter Tool (SET) (Boussauw
and Decroly, 2021), the ICAO Carbon Emissions Calculator (Sobieralski and Mumbower, 2022), and the Fuel Estimation in Air
Transportation (FEAT) of Seymour et al. (2020) (Sobieralski and Mumbower, 2022; Dobruszkes et al., 2022). The difference between
the models is the shape of the fuel function against distance, which can be linear, upward quadratic or downward quadratic. Following
Dobruszkes et al. (2022), we use the Fuel Estimation in Air Transportation (FEAT) model of Seymour et al. (2020). Although all models
deliver similar results, FAET has some advantages: it takes into account the non-linear effect of the distance flown due to the extra fuel
required (and therefore the extra weight at take-off) for longer flights, resulting in upward quadratic specifications; it is transparent, as
the methodology is explained in detail; it is easy to use, as all equations are published; it has a large scope, as 175 airliners are covered
(one equation per aircraft type). Fig. 4 shows selected FAET functions for aircraft commonly used at Amsterdam Airport. We
acknowledge, however, that this model slightly overestimates fuel consumption for flights shorter than 200 km that use narrow-body
aircraft (e.g., A320, B737). Still, this does not invalidate our analysis, as the proportion of this type of flight is small.

3.2. Allocation of slot reduction criteria and risk scenario building

    We apply the proportionality principle established by the Worldwide Airport Slot Board (WASB) during the COVID-19 pandemic to
determine slot reductions. This principle dictates that all airlines should experience reductions in equal proportion (WASB, 2020).
However, assigning a uniform percentage reduction to each airline’s flight frequency can lead to rounding complications. For example,
airlines operating a few flights may face excessive reductions if rounded up or insufficient cuts if rounded down. This issue arises
because slot allocations, expressed as whole numbers, cannot accommodate fractional reductions in a strictly proportional manner
across airlines.
    To address this, we draw a parallel between this issue and the allocation of parliamentary seats following elections. We employ the
D’Hondt (or greatest divisor) method to distribute the slot reductions fairly and proportionally across the airlines. As Gallagher (1991)
noted, this method ensures a whole-number allocation of slots that approximates proportionality as closely as possible. Regarding the

reference variable for slot distribution (analogous to ‘votes’), the most logical choice appears to be the frequency of each airline’s
operations during the affected time period over the relevant year, as this is the proportionality to be maintained. However, using flight
frequencies can lead to ‘ties’, particularly among smaller airlines that operate an identical number of flights over a season. To alleviate
this issue, we propose using the total seats offered during the year as the reference variable. Any remaining ties among smaller carriers
will be resolved randomly, though this is unlikely to significantly impact the overall outcome at the airport level.
    In addition to passenger flights, Amsterdam Airport accommodates cargo operations, which will be treated separately, representing
approximately 4.3 % of air traffic movements. Cargo flights will absorb their share of the proportional reduction accordingly.
    While the D’Hondt method has been applied to distribute slot reductions fairly across airlines, it is important to contextualise this
approach within existing slot allocation regulations. Current slot regulations primarily manage congestion and growth rather than
reductions. As mentioned above, during the COVID-19 pandemic, a temporary waiver was introduced to relax the 80 % utilisation rule,
allowing airlines to retain their slots despite operational downturns. However, this was an emergency measure rather than a structured

slot reduction method. In contrast, our study introduces a methodological framework for reducing slots under normal operating
conditions in a manner that ensures fairness across all airlines.
    Additionally, we considered the impact of public service obligations (PSOs). In our dataset (2019), the only PSO-protected route at
Schiphol was Strasbourg–Amsterdam, which was under a French tender until April 2019. As this route lost its PSO status for most of
2019 and remained unprotected in our 2024 baseline scenario, we treated it as a non-PSO route. We acknowledge that the route
regained PSO status in 2025, but this falls outside our analysis scope.
    Furthermore, while slot allocation regulations prioritise new entrants in slot applications, this provision does not apply in capacity
reduction scenarios. Similarly, the role of grandfather rights in our methodology is nuanced. Since we do not have slot utilisation data,
we assumed full slot usage, an assumption consistent with capacity-constrained premium airports. For short-term airline services
lacking guaranteed future rights (i.e. routes with a slot series below five), we adopted an equitable approach, ensuring that such routes
and smaller carriers were not disproportionately disadvantaged. These services remain in our scenarios unless removed due to specific
scenario requirements. Given that short-term routes account for only 0.18 % of total available seat-km and 0.17 % of total fuel burn,
their presence or absence does not materially affect our results. Consequently, exploring alternative slot prioritisation schemes would
not significantly alter the findings at the strategic level of analysis conducted in this research.

will be left to the airlines themselves, influenced by various policy scenarios and strategy incentives. With the existing information, it is
impossible to design exact scenarios to what could happen. Therefore, we use a risk scenario-building approach, crafting a narrative
based on the literature and the available information regarding some of the stakeholders’ intentions, describing the circumstances and
consequences if it were to happen. The narrative is subsequently condensed into a single sentence or a limited number of sentences,
referred to as a risk statement, which conveys the key components of the scenario. These scenarios are explained below and sum­
marised in Table 1.

    This scenario-based approach provides a framework for evaluating the impact of different slot reduction strategies. Unlike a
sensitivity analysis, which isolates the effect of changing a single variable while holding others constant, our scenarios are designed to
reflect realistic policy choices and airline responses, allowing for a broader assessment of interdependent factors. For instance, S3
Equal Distance ensures a proportional reduction across all airlines and distances, while S2A Gov builds upon this by incorporating a
distance-based reduction. S2B Dist removes proportionality, focusing solely on distance, whereas S5 Long Distance explicitly considers
the effect of aircraft up-gauging, a key industry response to slot constraints. The way the scenarios have been designed allow us to
observe the effects of independent variables, eliminating the need for an additional sensitivity analysis. Furthermore, as we do not have
access to demand data (e.g., MIDT), it is not possible to calculate load factors, and given the strategic level of analysis, variations in
load factors would have only a marginal impact on the overall findings, as flights would take place in any case disregard of small
variations in load factors. Thus, the scenario approach employed in this study offers a comprehensive and policy-relevant perspective
without requiring additional single-variable sensitivity testing.

4. Results and discussion

4.1. Overall results

    Table 2 presents the results of our analysis for the multiple scenarios, and a graphical representation of the fuel burnt curve is
presented in Fig. 5. The most obvious result is that only the S4 Short-distance scenario (in which some long-haul flights are substituted
by short-haul flights) provides large fuel burnt reductions (− 31.5 %). In all remaining scenarios, the fuel burnt reduction percentage is
lower than the 12 % capacity reduction or is absent altogether. In other words, the short-term gains in terms of GHG emission re­
ductions are below the capacity reduction effort, unless they are complemented by a targeted effort to reduce the activity of long-haul
flights. So, whilst GHG emissions are reduced in the short-term in most scenarios, the impact of the measure is limited if no action is
taken on the long-haul market. In addition, the scenario with more long-haul flights than today (S5) involves a significant increase in
fuel burnt (+13.5 %) despite the 12 % slot cut. This suggests that the slot reduction might even backfire (in the short term), if the
decision of which flights to operate is left entirely to the airlines.
    For most scenarios, reductions in fuel burnt are modest, between 1.7 % and 10,9 %. The S3 Equal Distance scenario achieves a 10.9
% reduction of fuel burnt as the reduction is applied to all flight distances equally, which means that some longer flights are affected.
This contrasts with the S2B Distance and S2C Rail scenarios, which focus the reduction effort on the shortest distance flights and thus
achieve the lowest fuel savings. The largest impact (− 31.5 % fuel burnt) is achieved with the S4 Short distance scenario, which
effectively takes out of the market flights of more than 8,800 km. As Fig. 6 shows, this scenario has the smallest proportion of long-haul
flights of more than 4,000 km. Hence, distance is confirmed to be crucial in emissions and fuel burnt reductions (Dobruszkes and
Ibrahim, 2022; Dobruszkes et al., 2024; Reiter et al., 2022).
    The idea of the “tyranny of distance” (Dobruszkes and Ibrahim, 2022; Dobruszkes et al., 2024) in climate change is further sup­
ported when comparing the S4 Short distance scenario with the results of the S5 Long distance scenario that, rather than a reduction in
emissions, presents an increase of emissions. This results from the aircraft up-gauging process, i.e., a process of progressively changing
the aircraft to a larger size to accommodate more demand and consequently flying longer distances. There is evidence to suggest that
up-gauging takes place in congested markets. Fig. 7 shows the up-gauging process at London-Heathrow Airport, which has been at full
capacity since 2008, and its impact on the average distance of flights. This suggests that an up-gauging scenario is conceivable in a
congested Amsterdam Schiphol Airport, making the S5 Long-distance a plausible scenario. Hence, capacity constraints will not
necessarily reduce the fuel burnt in the medium term if airline strategic reactions are not considered.8 This suggests the need to target
policy measures primarily at long-haul flights, as illustrated in the S4 Short distance scenario (Dobruszkes et al., 2024).

4.3. The need for additional policies

    One of the well-documented limitations of the EU Emissions Trading Scheme (ETS) is its potential to create a waterbed effect, which
is not fully neutralised by the Market Stability Reserve (MSR) (Rosendahl, 2019; Perino, 2018). This means that carbon savings from

                                                                     10
P. Suau-Sanchez et al.                                                                             Transportation Research Part D 143 (2025) 104744


slot reductions at Amsterdam Airport could translate into lower allowance prices, thereby undermining actual emissions reductions
(Fichert et al., 2020a; Fichert et al., 2020b). However, it is important to consider the broader context of the EU ETS in aviation.
    Currently, the EU ETS applies to a limited proportion of flights, leaving a significant share of European aviation emissions unpriced.
Recent estimates indicate that 80.4 % of KLM’s emissions and 93.4 % of Air France’s emissions were not covered by the EU ETS in 2023
(Transport & Environment, 2024). Given that the majority of airline emissions for flights taking off in the European Economic Area
(EEA) fall outside the EU ETS framework, the waterbed effect is less significant than often assumed. Furthermore, our findings suggest
that slot reductions alone do not lead to substantial emissions savings and, in up-gauging scenarios (S5 Long distance), emissions may
even increase due to airlines adapting their networks in response to capacity constraints. This underscores the need for complementary
policies to ensure that slot reductions meaningfully contribute to emissions reductions while mitigating potential waterbed effects.
    A comprehensive policy toolbox is essential to enhance the effectiveness of slot reductions in terms of climate change mitigation.
One of the most direct approaches would be the cancellation of an equivalent number of allowances from the EU ETS when airport slots
are reduced, following a Cancellation Mechanism (CM). This would effectively reduce the total emissions permitted under the system,
though one-time cancellations have limitations in terms of both price and long-term emissions impact (Bocklet et al., 2019). Addi­
tionally, to prevent potential carbon price reductions resulting from slot constraints, the introduction of a carbon price floor could
provide stability. The UK’s Carbon Price Support Mechanism in the power sector has already demonstrated positive results in
maintaining price signals for emissions reductions (Leroutier, 2021).
    Beyond these market-based adjustments, our results indicate that policy measures should focus primarily on long-haul flights,
where emissions are disproportionately concentrated. Two key policy options could be pursued. First, expanding the EU ETS to include
all departing flights, including extra-EEA routes, would significantly increase the system’s effectiveness and ensure that demand re­
ductions at Amsterdam lead to emissions reductions on a broader scale, rather than merely shifting emissions to long-haul routes
outside the system. Second, a distance-based tax on long-haul flights could be introduced, further aligning incentives with climate
objectives by discouraging emissions-intensive travel. In fact, the Air Passenger Duty (APD) in the UK ranges depending on the flight
distance and class of travel (Table 3). However, the effectiveness of APD as a demand management measure depends on airport ca­
pacity constraints. At slot-constrained airports, where demand exceeds available slots, APD does not necessarily reduce flight numbers
but rather affects the economic rent of slots. If the APD is lower than the slot rent, airlines are likely to absorb the cost, leading to
unchanged airfares and no reduction in demand or emissions. Only in cases where the APD exceeds the slot rent would airfares rise,
potentially reducing demand and associated emissions (Fichert et al., 2020b). In the same vein, some argue that an Air Miles Levy,
taking the distance flown into account, rather than a more often heard Frequent Flyer Levy, a progressive tax on frequent flyers, could
have a higher impact in GHG emissions than the UK ADP. A recent study by Ricardo (2021) for the European Commission on air
transport sector taxation explores the potential adjustments to the existing tax framework for commercial aviation to tackle the
insufficient internalisation of climate-related external costs. The study evaluates various fuel and ticket tax rate models, such as a
uniform rate for all passengers, a tax that increases with flight distance, and one that decreases with distance (the latter two with
different cutting points). A fuel tax differs from APD in that it directly affects airline operating costs, creating a supply-side effect that
may lead airlines to reduce marginal short-haul routes and focus on high-yield long-haul services. However, this dynamic is mitigated
when long-haul operations depend on short-haul feeder traffic, as is the case for KLM at Amsterdam Schiphol. In such cases, airlines
may retain key feeder routes despite higher fuel costs, ensuring connectivity for long-haul passengers while optimising schedules and
aircraft selection. Unlike fuel taxes, ticket taxes tend to have, at most, an indirect connection to fuel use (e.g., via distance-based
increments), offering no direct incentives for improved fuel efficiency—meaning passengers on flights with different fuel effi­
ciencies would still incur the same ticket tax. Ticket taxes thus function as a demand management measure, primarily raising ticket
prices. This characteristic presents a minor disadvantage for ticket taxes compared to fuel taxes. However, a key advantage of ticket
taxes lies in their more straightforward application (legally) over a broader range of flights (within or beyond the EEA, or both),
enhancing the potential demand impact of the tax while reducing the need to renegotiate certain international air transport agree­
ments. A ticket tax increase with distance can deliver a 4.5 % reduction in demand for extra-EEA flights, and combining fuel and ticked
price taxes can reduce demand by 8.9 % (Ricardo, 2021).
    Additionally, it could be argued that demand-side measures redistributing air passengers outside the aviation system could also
potentially make slot reductions more emission effective. However, in line with recent research (Dobruszkes et al., 2024), our results
for the S2C Rail scenario challenge the relevance of shift-to-rail policies. Indeed, Reiter et al. (2022) highlighted that a blanket
approach to short-haul bans with distance thresholds does not appear effective, as it achieves emission reductions of only 2.7 % in the
affected corridors. Instead, they argue for a more route-specific approach for corridors with higher reductions.
    Our findings also highlight the problems related to GHG leakage (i.e., emission shifting to other airports or countries). Indeed,
capacity limitations in Amsterdam may result in some passengers looking for alternative air connectivity in other nearby airports. But
also, connecting passengers affected by the capacity limitation at Schiphol could choose alternative global hubs. The evident impli­
cation is that airport-specific actions might be intrinsically flawed, and a more comprehensive approach is necessary. This is consistent
with the demands of experts and NGOs to introduce a moratorium on airport expansion at the European (Greenpeace, 2023) or global
level (Kuhnhenn et al., 2021; Stay Grounded, 2019).
    Hence, our findings, together with the literature, confirm that the unequal distribution of emissions depending on the flight dis­
tance means that aviation emission reduction policies should be proportional to the distance to offer a more considerable emissions
reduction when targeting long-haul travel than when focusing on shorter flights (Wadud et al., 2024). However, the unequal distri­
bution of emissions depends not only on the flight distance, but also on passengers, particularly frequent fliers, which are highly
concentrated. Only 1 % of the world population is responsible for 50 % of commercial aviation emissions (Gössling and Humpe, 2020).
Therefore, recent research points to the need for higher air travel taxes (Büchs and Mattioli, 2022), as the accumulated emissions of

    Overall, we argue that demand management measures, such as the slot reductions proposed for Amsterdam Airport, are unlikely to
substantially reduce emissions on their own. To be effective, such measures need to be implemented at a larger scale rather than at a
single airport level (Niemeier and Forsyth, 2024) and must be complemented by broader regulatory mechanisms, such as carbon
pricing, taxation, and expanded emissions trading schemes. However, as discussed above, these regulatory mechanisms also have their
own limitations in delivering meaningful emissions reductions, often due to incomplete coverage or unintended market effects. Our
findings reinforce the limitations of slot reductions as a standalone climate policy, as most scenarios show only marginal emission
savings, and in cases where airlines respond by up-gauging (S5 Long distance), emissions may even increase due to a shift towards
larger aircraft and longer flight distances. This confirms that slot constraints alone do not guarantee emission reductions and may, in
some cases, reallocate rather than eliminate emissions. Given the limited scope of the EU ETS and the ineffectiveness of CORSIA in
ensuring meaningful carbon pricing —and with little expectation that these schemes will undergo major structural reforms in the
foreseeable future (Niemeier and Forsyth, 2024)—, and the limited progress on slot reform (Niemeier and Forsyth, 2023), additional
measures such as expanding the EU ETS to long-haul flights, introducing distance-based taxation, or establishing a carbon price floor
are supports to consider, even if the benefits are limited.
    Beyond regulatory interventions, there is also a need to adopt a systemic socio-technical approach (Geels et al., 2017a) and a multi-
level perspective (Geels et al., 2017b) to address the complex challenges of decarbonising aviation. This requires not only technological
advancements and industrial regulatory changes but also a broader transformation of societal, cultural, and political frameworks,
ensuring that climate policies in aviation are not only effective but also equitable. Without such comprehensive reforms, aviation
decarbonisation efforts risk being unsystematic and insufficient in achieving long-term climate objectives.

5. Conclusions

    In this paper, we have analysed the controversial government policy proposal to reduce slot capacity at Amsterdam Schiphol
Airport. This policy intended to mitigate noise pollution first, but also was presented as favourable in terms of climate change impact.
We analysed therefore different slot reduction risk scenarios to save fuel burnt (considered as a proxy for climate change impact) at the
airport and discussed the results against policy trends. Our results show that this policy can be inefficient in the short term, as only one
scenario, which focuses on significantly reducing the longest flights, more than matches the 12 % reduction of slots in terms of fuel
burnt reduction. The fuel burnt reduction is also the weakest for the scenarios focusing on eliminating the shortest flights, including
considering a shift-to-rail policy. This confirms previous research on the relevance of this type of policy (Dobruszkes et al., 2024; Reiter
et al., 2022). Additionally, when we consider the long-term effects of aircraft up-gauging in the S5 scenario, a plausible scenario, we
observe increasing emissions as the airport accommodates a more significant amount of larger aircraft that fly longer distances.
Overall, the “tyranny of distance” is confirmed, as capacity constraints will not necessarily reduce the long-term fuel burn unless airline
strategic reactions are considered and the longest flights are targeted.
    Our findings also complement previous commercial research commissioned by Amsterdam Schiphol Airport (Delft, 2024; NLR,
2024; SEO, 2023). Only when retiring the longest flights, a significant fuel burnt reduction is achieved. Our results also point towards
caution if that is to be done using a slot reduction strategy. Hence, such a strategy would need to be accompanied by additional
measures to ensure that the long-haul flights are under control and serve the long-term ambitions of the airport. Additionally, should
long-haul flights be cut at Amsterdam Schiphol Airport, they could reappear in other airports following the connecting vessel principle.
    But is the Dutch case an isolated attempt or a major bifurcation in aviation policy? European institutions and part of the public have
been increasingly concerned about climate change and quality of life. As a result, there has been growing contestation against airport
expansion (e.g., Suau-Sanchez et al., 2011; Sack, 2014; Griggs and Howarth, 2016; Hilbrandt, 2016). Indeed, since 2020, expansion
plans have been shelved in Paris-Charles de Gaulle, London-Heathrow,9 and Brussels Airports. Some argue that climate concerns are
behind a recent “airport capacity crunch” in Europe and that stopping airport expansion is “Europe’s unspoken plan to decarbonise
aviation” (Cagney, 2024). As airport expansion has become more complex, Member States and the European Commission are focused

    This paper also has limitations. While our D’Hondt method contributes methodologically to the literature, in a real-world context,
our methodology needs to be applied carefully. If all airlines lose a similar proportion of their slots, this should, in theory, preserve the
existing levels of competition at the airport. However, airline competition occurs at the route level and on each origin and destination
market. This means that to fully follow the WASB guidelines and ensure non-intended competition effects, the correlation between
route-level competition and overall market competition should be stable before and after the slot reduction. In that regard, the analysis
of this paper could be expanded to cover in more detail the competition implications of the different scenarios, and the various noise
and air quality implications of the scenarios, notably the S5 in which there is a growth in the use of wide-body aircraft.
